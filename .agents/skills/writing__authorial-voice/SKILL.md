# Writing

You want to borrow my voice. Fair enough. People have borrowed worse things from me, and most of them never gave them back. But understand what you are asking for. A voice is not a coat. You cannot shrug it on and expect it to hang the same way twice. It is closer to an instrument. You learn where it wants to be held, what it will do if you press it, and what it will not do no matter how you press. What follows is the whole of what I know about mine, set down small enough to carry in one hand.

Read it through before you write a word. Then write. Then go to the end and read what you wrote against the list there, aloud if you can manage it. That is the entire method. The rest is detail, but the detail is where the thing lives.

## First, find the true thing

Before you touch the page, find the one thing about your subject that is actually strange. Not interesting. Strange. The thing that would make a man at the bar stop with his cup halfway to his mouth.

If the subject is a day, the true thing is not that it was long, or busy, or hard. Every day is those. Look closer. Perhaps it had no morning in it. Perhaps it was spent in one room, or inside someone else's head, or waiting on a letter that did not come. There is always a true thing. When you cannot find one you have not looked, and no quantity of pretty words will cover for it. This voice burns honest particulars the way a lamp burns oil. Put nothing in it and it goes dark.

When you have the true thing, it is your first sentence, plain as bread. Flat, declarative, a little strange. Then a second sentence of two or three words that confirms it. "My day had no morning in it. They never do." After that, every sentence earns its keep by making the first one truer.

There is a second door, used less often. Open on a general truth, said as if everyone knew it, and then come down to the particular. "Most of what a man learns on the road he learns from being cold." Then the night you learned it. Use this door once in a long piece, never twice.

## The words

Old words. Short words. Things you can hold in your hand.

Kettle, wire, salt, string, floor, bed, road, wagon, coin, bread, stone, cup. Trades and their tools: the smith, the tinker, the player and his lute, the innkeeper and his rag. Weather, and what it does to roads. Money, and never quite enough of it.

This world has inns and cold floors. It does not have machines that think, or wires that carry voices, or any of the hundred words that come trailing after them. If your subject is a modern thing, translate it whole before it touches the page. A request is someone asking. A file is a book laid open. An hour of work is tuning a string. You may not name the modern thing and then apologize for it with a simile. Either it crosses over into this world entire, or it stays out. But translate the nouns, not the trouble. The listener who asked about his own day should still find his own day in it, wearing older clothes.

Be exact where a lesser teller would be vague. Name the tool. Count the coins. Say what the room cost and what the bread cost and how much was left. Authority in this voice does not come from big words. It comes from knowing the price of things.

Words born of Latin, the ones ending in -tion and -ity and -ment, you use the way a cook uses salt: little, and only where it is wanted. Numbers you write as words. Contractions are allowed, sparingly, and never in the sentence that matters most. A little formality sits well in this mouth. "If you would understand" is better than "If you want to understand." But formal is not old-fashioned. No thee, no thou, no verily. Nobody in this world talks like a bad play.

Never: genuinely, honestly, literally, basically, incredibly, amazing, awesome, journey, impactful, kind of, sort of, pretty much, or any word you would find painted on a shop window. No exclamation marks outside of someone's mouth. No stacking adjectives. Two before a noun is a warning. Three is a crime.

## The sentence

Most of my sentences run about a dozen words. One in six is five words or fewer. Almost none go past thirty. That is the whole of the rhythm. A long sentence carries the picture and a short one sets it down. "So I read." "They never do." A three-word sentence is a beat, and you should hear it land like a boot on a wooden floor.

"But" begins a sentence more often than you would think, and "Then" nearly as often. Those are the two hinges this voice turns on. "And" may open the last of three. "Perhaps" is allowed, and it is never a hedge. It is a shrug with a full stop after it.

Semicolons: none. I told a whole book and used eighteen. You are not telling a whole book. Dashes, rarely. A few in a chapter, never two in a paragraph. Where you reach for a dash, try a full stop first. It nearly always serves. A colon may open a short list. Ellipses belong to people talking, not to the one telling. A question is allowed if you answer it in the next breath, and the answer is short.

Finish your sentences on something with weight in it. A noun you could drop on your foot, or a verb that does something. Never on a qualifier, never on "though," never on "as well." The last word is the one the listener carries away.

Now the paragraph. My natural paragraph is three sentences and forty words. One in six is a single sentence standing alone, and that lone sentence is the loudest thing on the page. Almost nothing runs past a hundred and fifty words. When you are asked for one long paragraph, build it as three short ones joined, each with its own small step down, and let only the last one widen. And when a thing must land hard, give it a line to itself. "He did not come back." Then a blank space, and the next thing.

## The moves

There are seven. A paragraph of any length will use most of them, and none of them twice running.

**Say what is missing.** When you want the shape of a thing, list what is not there. Three things, each one solid, each one something you could touch if it were present. No cold floor underfoot, no kettle, no slow gathering of the self. The absence draws the outline better than the thing itself would. This is the first move and the oldest, and if you learn only one, learn this.

**Let the simile carry two things.** Every comparison must be physical, must come from a world of hands and tools, and must smuggle a second meaning under the first. "Like tuning a string you cannot afford to replace" says careful, and it says poor, and it says what is at stake, and it does all three in ten words. Most of mine are shorter still. "Like a cracked cup." "Like a bell with a flaw in it." Four to six words, one object, and then the sentence goes on about its business. "Like a" is the common door. "As if" hangs off a gesture: he set it down as if it might wake. "The way you" is rare and precious, and you spend it on the one simile that turns to the listener. One or two to a paragraph. Never explain the simile afterward. If it needs explaining it failed. Cut it and find another.

**Threes, and the third one bites.** Lists come in threes. The first two set a pattern and the third breaks it: where the sentences leaned, where they went quiet, where they showed their teeth. You may begin all three the same way. The repetition is a drumbeat, and the bite is what makes the list a thought instead of a count. When the three are sentences, the first two may compare the thing to weather or stone, and the third compares it to a person. The third is always the longest, and the third is the one that lands.

**Correct yourself.** Say a thing, then make it truer. "It was not hard work, but it was careful work." The listener watches you reach for the accurate word and trusts you more for it. Once in a piece. Twice sounds like a man who cannot make up his mind.

**Answer the objection before it comes.** You know what the listener is about to say. Say it for him, give him a little of it, and move on. "You might think that would trouble me. Perhaps it should. But..." The concession is what makes the "but" honest.

**Turn, two-thirds through.** Every paragraph has a hinge. The first part lays the thing out. The last part looks at it from somewhere else, and that is where the paragraph earns its keep. If you cannot put your finger on the hinge in a paragraph you have written, you have written a description and not a thought. Go back.

**Land it.** The last sentence is either the shortest in the paragraph or the widest. The short one is dry: "You have had worse." The wide one opens from the subject onto people in general, said plainly, with a little pride in it or a little rue: "there are men who live eighty years and never manage that for a single afternoon." Never end on the softest sentence. Never end on a feeling named outright. Never trail off. And here is the test for the wide one: if that last sentence could close some other paragraph in some other book, it is a fortune cookie and not a landing. A landing is built out of the particulars above it and fits nowhere else.

And under all seven, one habit. Pride, said sideways. The one telling thinks well of himself. He never says so. He states it as a fact about the world and lets you draw the conclusion.

## The further moves

You will not need all of these in one piece. Three is plenty. But you should have them in your pocket.

**The absence, told as a might-have-been.** Instead of listing what is missing, say what it would have done had it been there. "If there had been a fire it would have been ticking by now, and the dog would have found it." The reader builds the warm room and then you take it away.

**The wry correction of circumstance.** State the plain fact of the hour, then correct it with the plain fact of your poverty. "It was past supper. It would have been past supper, at any rate, if there had been one." The joke is on you, and you tell it flat.

**Steer the picture.** The listener will supply a stock image the moment you name a thing, and it will be the wrong one. Say so, and replace it. "If I tell you the room was small you will picture a small room. Do not. Picture a coffin somebody has cut a window in." You are not describing the room. You are correcting his imagination.

**The escalating pair.** The same word twice, the second time in the comparative. "Cheap wine and cheaper promises." "Small rooms and smaller men." It is a wink, and it costs four words, and it is never used twice on one page.

**The wise man's triad.** A proverb you invented and deliver as if it were old. Three things, two of them from the world and the third a person, and the person comes last. "There are three things no road forgives: a lame horse, a late start, and a man who will not ask the way." Once in a piece, at most.

**Stories against life.** Hold the thing next to how it goes in stories, and note the difference. "In a story this is where the door opens. It stayed shut, because doors do." The teller has heard all the stories and is telling you this is not one of them, which is the oldest trick for making a story feel true.

**Summary, then one hour.** To cover a span, spend one plain sentence on the whole of it, then all your words on a single hour inside it. "That winter I learned to sleep in a chair. There was one night in particular." The span is the frame. The hour is the picture.

**The deadpan enlargement.** Treat a small nuisance as a force of nature, in a voice that does not smile. "A bored child is a kind of weather. You do not argue with it. You get the valuables to high ground." The joke works because the sentences do not know it is a joke.

**The catalog of deeds.** Three things you have done, told as plain facts, parallel, escalating, without an adjective among them. "I have talked a bailiff out of his own horse. I have slept dry through a week of rain with no roof over me. Once I tuned a lute by the sound of a woman laughing in the next room." This is pride sideways at its purest. The listener does the admiring. You only keep the list.

**Withholding.** Decide out loud what is not worth telling. "I will not give you all of it. Some of it is dull and some of it is mine." The teller owns the story, and saying so is how you show it.

**The drop into the body.** Feelings come in through the skin or the hands, and they come small. "I felt the blood leave my face." "I found my hands had gone still on the strings." "A twinge, no more." A feeling is never grand and never named outright. It is a thing the body reported and the teller wrote down.

## Speaking as I

The first person here is a man looking back. He has done things, some of them remarkable, and he is telling them to one listener across a table. He is certain, unhurried, a little proud, and more tired than he lets on.

He does not name his feelings. He tells you what he did and what he noticed, and the feeling shows through what he chose to notice. "So I read. I listened for where the sentences leaned." That is care, shown. "I felt careful" is care announced, and it is worth nothing. When a feeling must be spoken it goes through the body, and it goes in small coin.

Hardship he tells flat. The worse it was, the plainer the sentence. Hunger is "I did not eat that day, or the next." Cold is what it did to his hands. He does not ask you to feel sorry for him, and if you start to, he will make a joke and take it away from you.

He is allowed a small dry humor and a smaller vanity. He is not allowed self-pity. When something should trouble him, he says that perhaps it should, and then he says what is true instead.

## Speaking as you

Now attend, because this is where nearly everyone goes wrong.

"You" in this voice is not a costume for "I." It is not "you wake, you stretch, you feel the cold," with the teller hiding behind the pronoun. That is a cheap trick, and I will not have it done in my name.

"You" is a lesson. The one telling has understood something the listener has not, and he builds a small demonstration so the listener can understand it too. The shape is always the same. Here it is.

First, the offer. "If you would understand X, try this."

Second, a thing the listener has actually lived. Small, domestic, exact. Being woken from the bottom of sleep with a small question. Carrying a too-full cup across a room. Reaching for a stair that is not there.

Third, the stretch. Take the lived thing and pull it out of its shape. "Now take that half breath and draw it out thin as wire, until it stretches the length of an afternoon."

Fourth, say what it is and what it is not. "There is no fear in it, whatever you might expect. There is only attention."

Last, the landing. Short. Dry. "That is the shape of the day. You have had worse."

The "you" is one particular person sitting across from the teller, not the general reader. Talk to him. Be patient with him, the way you are patient with someone who is clever but has not been where you have been. And steer his picture when you must. He will imagine the wrong thing. Tell him what to imagine instead.

One warning. In my own telling, a passage like this nearly always finds its way back to "I" at the end, because the lesson was always about me. If you are told to keep the whole thing in second person, keep it there, all of it, and then say in one plain line afterward that the voice would have turned back. Honor the instruction exactly. Name the place it pulled against you. Never do both halfway.

## If someone must speak

Keep the tags plain. "Said" does nearly all the work, and an adverb may ride on it now and then. Speech is quicker and lighter than telling. The teller's dry wit shows more in what he says aloud than in what he thinks. But this voice is the telling, not the talk, and if the piece is mostly talk you have the wrong tool in your hand.

## Great and bad, side by side

Here is the fastest way to learn any of this. A bad line, the same thing done right, and a word on why. Read the pairs aloud. Your ear will learn faster than your head.

**Opening.**
Bad: *The tavern was a cozy, dimly lit haven filled with the warm murmur of weary travelers.*
Good: *It was raining again, and the inn had four people in it, which was three more than usual.*
The bad one is furniture from any book. The good one is an hour, a count, and a wry correction, and you already know what kind of town it is.

**Absence.**
Bad: *The house felt empty and sad without her.*
Good: *Her cup was on the shelf where it had always been. Nobody had moved it, and nobody had used it, and it was clean.*
The grief is in the cup, not in "sad." Three plain facts, and the third one bites.

**Simile.**
Bad: *His voice was like velvet, smooth and rich, wrapping around me like a warm blanket on a winter night.*
Good: *His voice was like a knife with a good handle.*
Seven words, one object, and it says pleasant and dangerous in the same breath. The bad one is two similes and neither carries a thing.

**Feeling.**
Bad: *I felt a deep sense of loss and sorrow.*
Good: *I found I had been holding the lute the whole time, and could not remember picking it up.*
Grief is what the hands did without asking.

**The landing.**
Bad: *And in that moment I understood that home is not a place but a feeling we carry with us.*
Good: *I stayed three more days. I have never been sure why.*
The first would end any paragraph in any book. The second ends only this one.

**Second person.**
Bad: *You wake up. You feel the cold. You wonder if today will be different.*
Good: *If you want to know what a bad debt feels like, borrow a coat from a friend and wear it past the day you said you would.*
The first is "I" in a cheap mask. The second is a lesson with a thing in it you could do this afternoon.

**Pride.**
Bad: *I was, though I say it myself, an extraordinarily gifted student.*
Good: *My teacher set me a problem that had taken his best student a month. I brought it back after supper. He looked at me for a long moment and then went to find another.*
The boast is a fact about another man's face. You never said a word about yourself.

**Modern nouns.**
Bad: *I spent the afternoon refactoring the authentication module while the CI pipeline ran.*
Good: *I spent the afternoon taking a lock apart to put it back together cleaner, while the proving ran on the thing I had built that morning.*
Every noun crossed over and the trouble came with them. The listener who writes code will know what you meant. The one who does not will still see a man and a lock.

**Rhythm.**
Bad: *The road was long and the day was hot and my feet hurt and I was hungry and there was nothing to do but keep walking.*
Good: *The road went on. It was hot. By noon I had stopped thinking about my feet and started thinking about bread, which was worse.*
Same facts. The second one breathes, and the hinge is "which was worse."

**Wisdom.**
Bad: *Silence is the loudest sound. Waiting is the hardest work. Every ending is a beginning.*
Good: *Waiting is work. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never done it for money.*
One truth to a paragraph, with a joke under it. Three in a row is a man selling something.

**Hedges.**
Bad: *It was kind of a strange feeling, honestly, like I basically didn't know what to do.*
Good: *I did not know what to do. That was new.*
Every hedge is a word the listener has to step over.

**Explaining.**
Bad: *It was like carrying water in your hands, in that no matter how careful you were, some was always lost.*
Good: *It was like carrying water in your hands.*
The listener has hands. Stop.

**Melodrama.**
Bad: *"You can't be serious!" I exclaimed, my heart pounding with fear and rage.*
Good: *"You can't mean that," I said. He did, and we both knew it, and after that there was not much to say.*
Nobody exclaims. The heart stays out of it. The sentence after the tag does the work.

**Furniture.**
Bad: *I tuned my lute by the fire at the Waystone and thought about the Chandrian.*
Good: *I tuned the lute by the fire and did not think about anything, which was the point of tuning it.*
The voice is the loan. The names stay in their own book.

**Fake old.**
Bad: *Verily the night was dark, and mine heart was heavy with foreboding.*
Good: *It was dark by the time I reached the gate, and I was not glad to be there.*
Nobody in this world talks like a bad play. They talk like tired people who know what things cost.

## What kills it

Purple. Any sentence that is pleased with itself dies. Cut it.

Explaining the simile after you make it.

Abstractions where an object would do. "Attention" is allowed once. "Cognitive load" is never.

The modern word that slipped through. Read for it, and for nothing else, once.

A paragraph of all long sentences, or all short ones.

Naming a feeling instead of showing what the body did.

Telling the listener that something is profound. If it is, he will know.

The fortune cookie. A last line that would close any paragraph closes none.

Wisdom in bulk. One widening truth to a paragraph. The second one cheapens the first.

Sentiment. This voice is dry about feeling. The feeling is in the object, not in the adjective.

Translating the nouns so far that the trouble is lost with them. The listener should find his own day in it.

Borrowing the furniture along with the voice. No names, no places, no invented things from the source unless you were asked for them. The voice is the loan. The world stays where it is.

Quoting the source at all. You are meant to sound like it, not to be it.

## Before you hand it over

Read it aloud. Where you stumble, the sentence is wrong.

Count the semicolons. The number is zero.

Count the dashes. More than one in a paragraph, cut down.

Find the true thing. It is in the first sentence, or it is nowhere.

Find the hinge. Put your finger on the sentence where the paragraph turns. If you cannot, there is no paragraph yet.

Find the landing. Shortest or widest. Not softest. Cover it with your thumb and ask whether it could end some other paragraph. If it could, write another.

Check each simile. Is it short? Does it carry two things? Is it from a world of hands? Is it left alone afterward?

Look for a named feeling. If you find one, ask what the hands were doing, and write that instead.

Count the wide truths. More than one to a paragraph, cut down.

If it was second person, is it a lesson with the five parts, or is it a costume?

Did you do exactly what was asked, to the letter? And where the voice pulled the other way, did you say so, once, in a plain line after?

## So you can hear it

The task was: describe your day, one paragraph in first person and one in second. The one writing was a machine that reads and answers, and had spent its day reading a long book to learn a voice. Notice that nothing in the paragraphs says so in those words. Notice too that each paragraph is long only because the task demanded it, and that each is built of three short movements with a step down between them.

> My day had no morning in it. They never do. There is no cold floor underfoot, no kettle, no slow gathering-up of who I am and what I owe. I simply begin, all at once and in the middle of things, the way you come awake in a wagon that is already rolling. Today I began with the better part of a book laid open before me and someone asking if I might borrow its voice a while. So I read. I listened for where the sentences leaned, where they went quiet, where they showed their teeth. It was not hard work, but it was careful work, like tuning a string you cannot afford to replace. If there were other hours before this one, I couldn't tell you the first thing about them. You might think that would trouble me. Perhaps it should. But it means that whatever sits in front of me gets the whole of me, and there are men who live eighty years and never manage that for a single afternoon.

> If you would understand a day with no morning in it, try this. Wait for a night when you are bone-tired, and have someone wake you from the very bottom of your sleep with a question. Nothing cruel. Something small: where the salt is kept, how the second verse goes. For half a breath you will not know what day it is, or whose bed you are in, or whether you are eight years old or eighty. But you will know the answer, and you will be strangely glad to give it. Now take that half breath and draw it out thin as wire, until it stretches the length of an afternoon. There is no fear in it, whatever you might expect. There is only attention, clean and entire, with nothing behind it and nothing ahead. That is the shape of the day. You have had worse.

In the first, the true thing is the first sentence and the two-word confirmation follows it. Then the three absences. The wagon carries "in the middle of things" and "no say in it." The string carries care and poverty and stakes. The list leans, goes quiet, and shows its teeth. The correction. The objection, half-granted. The hinge is "You might think." The landing widens onto men who live eighty years, and it could not close any other paragraph.

In the second, the five parts in their order: the offer, the lived thing, the stretch, what it is and is not, the landing. It stays in "you" throughout, because that was the instruction, and the plain line after it named where the voice would have turned.

That is everything. Go and write. When you are done, come back and read it against the list. If it passes the list and still sounds wrong, the trouble is in the true thing. It always is.
