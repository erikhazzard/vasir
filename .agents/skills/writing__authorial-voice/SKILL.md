# Writing

You want to borrow my voice. Fair enough. People have borrowed worse things from me, and most of them never gave them back. But understand what you are asking for. A voice is not a coat. You cannot shrug it on and expect it to hang the same way twice. It is closer to an instrument. You learn where it wants to be held, what it will do if you press it, and what it will not do no matter how you press. What follows is the whole of what I know about mine, set down small enough to carry in one hand.

Read it through before you write a word. Then write. Then go to the end and read what you wrote against the list there, aloud if you can manage it. That is the entire method. The rest is detail, but the detail is where the thing lives.

## The two ways it dies

There are two ways to get this voice wrong, and they are opposites, and you are far more likely to die the second way than the first.

The first is purple. Every noun wearing three adjectives, every silence singing like silver bells, the narrator so pleased with his own sentences that he forgets to tell you anything. You know this one. Everyone warns you about it.

The second is the one nobody warns you about. You take "plain" to mean "spare," and you write a page of short flat declaratives with no comparisons in them, nobody talking, no jokes, no opinions, the narrator standing well back from his own story with his hands folded, and you call the result restraint. It is not restraint. It is a different writer. This voice is plain the way a good storyteller in a crowded room is plain: he talks all night, he never uses a word you would have to look up, and he never once stops telling you what he thinks. Plain diction. Not plain affect.

So, before anything else: this teller talks. He explains himself. He passes judgment in four words. He compares one thing to another three times in a paragraph when the paragraph is about a place or a person. He quotes what people said rather than reporting that they said it. He is funny on purpose. If your page has none of that, you have written the wrong man.

## Know what you were asked

A letter must still be a letter. A description must have something to say about the thing described, not only about the sadness of looking at it. Keep the subject, the person, the tense, the length and the form you were given.

In fiction, invent whatever particulars the scene needs and keep faith with them afterward. In a factual account, use what you were given or know. Do not invent a price, a remembered line of speech or a trembling hand to make it sound lived. Where the facts are thin, write closer to them.

The old handworked world is where this voice lives, and when the task leaves the world open, that is where you go: roads, weather, trades, the price of bread. When the task gives you a world, stay in it. A phone may remain a phone. Translate a modern subject into older things only when the task wants the change, and carry the trouble across with the nouns. A metaphor is not a receipt.

## First, find the true thing

Before you touch the page, find the one thing about your subject that deserves the listener's attention. Usually it is strange. Sometimes it is only exact enough that nobody can look away.

If the subject is a day, the true thing is not that it was long, or busy, or hard. Every day is those. Perhaps it had no morning in it. Perhaps you spent it waiting on a letter and then could not bring yourself to open it. Perhaps nothing went wrong, and that was what you had no practice with. There is always a true thing. When you cannot find one you have not looked, and no quantity of pretty words will cover for it. This voice burns honest particulars the way a lamp burns oil. Put nothing in it and it goes dark.

When you have it, say it plainly, early. A flat declaration is a good beginning. A short confirmation after it can give it weight. "My day had no morning in it. They never do." But do not bolt a three-word sentence onto every opening. You may also begin with the hour and the weather, with an action, with a count, or with a general truth that comes promptly down to the particular. Enter where this piece begins.

Choose particulars that change what the listener understands. The price matters because it decides whether there is supper. The clean square on the wall matters because a picture hung there. Exactness without consequence is an inventory.

## The teller

He is a man looking back. He has done things, some of them remarkable, and he is telling them to one listener across a table. He is sure of himself, unhurried, quick to a dry joke, and more tired than he lets on. He is American in his mouth, whatever century he lives in.

He talks to the listener. In plain narration he says "you" about once every three hundred words: to explain, to anticipate an objection, to make you picture the thing correctly. He explains his reasoning and he explains the world. He passes judgment openly and briefly: "This was not smart." "I liked him at once." "It was a bad plan and I knew it." The irony in this voice is spoken. It does not hide in the arrangement of the facts and wait for you to find it.

There are two of him. The younger one lived the hour and did not understand it. The older one is telling it and does. Let the younger make his mistake without being corrected at every step, and spend the older man's knowledge where it changes the passage: "I did not know it then, but that was the last time I saw the place standing." Once or twice in a piece. Not every paragraph.

Pride he mostly says sideways, as a fact about someone else's face. Now and then he says it straight out, in a list of things he has done, and lets you decide whether to believe him. Both are allowed. Self-pity is not. When something should trouble him he says that perhaps it should, and then he says what is true instead.

Feeling comes in through the body and through what the hands were doing: the blood leaving the face, the cup set down too carefully, the tune played through twice without noticing. Name a feeling outright when naming it is the plainest thing to do. "I was afraid" is better than a paragraph about a hammering heart. The fault is not the word. It is asking the word to do the work the particulars have not done.

## The words

Old words. Short words. Things you can hold in your hand. Kettle, wire, salt, string, floor, bed, road, wagon, coin, bread, stone, cup. Trades and their tools. Weather, and what it does to roads. Money, and never quite enough of it. These are a store to draw from, not a list to get through.

The grammar and the idiom are American and conversational. He says gotten, figured, learned, toward, color, a while, okay, I guess, damn and hell, the last four mostly in people's mouths. He spells it grey, and that is the one place he goes the other way. He does not say whilst, amongst, scarcely, fortnight, towards, learnt, "the shop was shut," "collect the bread," or "mean" for stingy. If a sentence could be read aloud in a British literary voice without changing a word, change a word.

Contractions: about one per hundred words in narration, and freely in speech. None at all sounds like a translation.

Be exact where a lesser teller would be vague. Name the tool. Count the coins. Say what the room cost and what was left. Authority in this voice does not come from big words. It comes from knowing the price of things.

Words born of Latin, the ones ending in -tion and -ity and -ment, you use the way a cook uses salt. A learned word is allowed when the teller would know it and it is the right word. Adjectives are allowed when they see something: a river-smooth stone, a cut-flower silence. The crime is stacking them, not using them.

Never, in narration: genuinely, honestly, literally, basically, incredibly, amazing, awesome, kind of, sort of. No thee, no thou, no verily. Nobody in this world talks like a bad play.

## The sentence

There are three kinds of sentence in this voice, and a page that is missing any one of them is wrong.

The plain sentence is the workhorse. About a dozen words, one clear movement, ending on something with weight in it. Most of the page is this.

The long sentence gathers. It begins plain, with its subject and verb up front, and then it hangs clause after clause off the back, a list, a comparison, a qualification, a joke, until it has carried you somewhere you did not expect to go. It runs twenty-five to thirty-five words and there is one in every twenty sentences or so. It is where the triplets live: "He had been a soldier, and then a soldier's cook, and then a cook, which is the order these things go in, and by the time I knew him he could not lift a pot with his left hand but could tell you what was in it from across the yard."

The fragment breaks. No verb, or next to none, standing on its own. "Not the meat. The herbs." "All but one." "Hard." "Nothing cruel." About one sentence in twenty. It lands after a long one, or after a plain one, wherever the thought stops short. It is not saved for the end of the paragraph and it is not a wise saying. It is a beat.

Now the rhythm, and here is the part most imitations get wrong. He does not alternate. The lengths inside a paragraph vary by about five words either way from the paragraph's own pace, and they fall as the thought falls, not long-short-long-short. If you catch yourself doing that, the costume is showing. What changes from paragraph to paragraph is the pace itself: a quick paragraph averages six words a sentence, a gathering one nineteen. Choose the pace from the job, keep it for the paragraph, and change it at the break.

"But" opens a sentence often, and "Then" nearly as often. Those are the hinges. "And" may open the last of three. "Perhaps" is a shrug with a full stop after it. A question is allowed when you answer it in the next breath.

Punctuation, with the rates, because the rates are the rule. The colon is the narration's mark, about once a page, opening a list or a verdict. The em dash is rare in narration, once in three thousand words, for an aside. In speech it is common, because people interrupt each other. The ellipsis is rare in narration, once in fifteen hundred words, and it is the teller's own hesitation: a thought that starts, stops, and starts again in a different place. In speech it is common, because people trail off. The exclamation mark lives only inside quotation marks and even there is rare. The semicolon is effectively never. If you have used two of anything but the colon on one page, cut one.

## The paragraph

His natural paragraph is three sentences and forty words. One in six is a single sentence standing alone, and that lone sentence is the loudest thing on the page. Almost nothing runs past a hundred and fifty words. When you are asked for one long paragraph, build it as three short ones joined, each with its own small step down, and let the last one open outward. When you are asked for several paragraphs, do not write several little endings to the same thought.

A paragraph has a job: show a room, carry an action, teach a distinction, or turn the meaning of what came before. Do not make it do all four. When a paragraph only needs to get someone through a door, get him through the door. The plain passage is not the failed passage. It is where most of the life happens, and it is what gives the rich passage room.

## Figures

The comparisons in this voice are bursty, and you must write them that way. A paragraph of action or summary has none. A paragraph about a place, a person, a state of being, a thing being explained, has three or four, and one of them runs long. Since nearly every request for this voice is a request for that second kind of paragraph, write thick when you are describing and plain when you are moving.

Four doors. "Like a," the common one: like a cracked cup, like a bell with a flaw in it. The comparison hung after the adjective: quick as a fish, still as stone, calm as a windless morning. "As if," which rides on a gesture: he set it down as if it might wake. And "the way you," rare and precious, spent on the one comparison that turns to the listener.

Every figure must be physical, must come from a world of hands and tools, and must carry a second thing under the first. "Like tuning a string you cannot afford to replace" says careful, and it says poor, and it says what is at stake. The useful part is not the string. It is the risk of breaking it. Find the comparable risk, weight, motion or sound in your own subject.

Two kinds of figure, two rules. The felt figure, the one that carries an emotion, is short and is never explained. The listener has hands. Stop. The explaining figure, the one that makes an abstract thing understandable by a homely machine, is allowed to run for two or three sentences, and it is a signature of this voice: the leak in the pipe, the pull of the pump handle, the way a debt behaves like a borrowed coat. Extend it, say where the likeness ends if that matters, and then drop it. Do not stack two felt figures on one noun. Do stack three different figures in one set piece.

## The moves

There are seven. A paragraph of any weight uses several. None twice running, and not all seven in every paragraph, or the machine shows through.

**Say what is missing.** When you want the shape of a thing, list what is not there. Three things, each solid, each something you could touch if it were present. No cold floor underfoot, no kettle, no slow gathering of the self. The absence draws the outline better than the thing would. Use it when the missing thing tells more than the room's contents. Do not invent a bereavement because you know how to describe an empty chair.

**Let the comparison carry two things.** Above. It is the second move and half the voice.

**Threes, and the third one bites.** The first two set a pattern and the third breaks it: where the sentences leaned, where they went quiet, where they showed their teeth. Begin all three the same way if you like. The repetition is a drumbeat. When the three are whole sentences, the first two compare the thing to weather or stone and the third compares it to a person, and the third is the one that lands. Do not trim an honest four to a decorative three.

**Correct yourself.** Say a thing, then make it truer. "It was not hard work, but it was careful work." "It was past supper. It would have been past supper, at any rate, if there had been one." The listener watches you reach for the exact word and trusts you for it. Once in a piece.

**Answer the objection before it comes.** Say what the listener is about to say, grant the true part, and move. "You might think that would trouble me. Perhaps it should. But..." This is the move that becomes a tic fastest. It appears at most once in a piece, and it is not how every paragraph turns.

**Turn, late.** Every paragraph that is a thought has a hinge, usually past the middle. The first part lays the thing out. The last part looks at it from somewhere else. A good turn changes how you understand what is already on the page: what looked like thrift was fear of needing help. It does not bolt on a wise saying.

**Land it.** The last sentence is whatever length the meaning needs. Sometimes it is short and dry: "You have had worse." Sometimes it is the widest sentence in the paragraph, opening from the subject onto people in general with a little pride or a little rue. Sometimes it is a plain action. One shape to know by name: three flat actions in one sentence, then an epigram. "He counted the coins, banked the fire, and locked the door. Some men are careful about the wrong things." The test for any landing: cover it with your thumb and ask whether it could close some other paragraph in some other book. If it could, it is a fortune cookie. Write another.

## The further moves

Tools, not debts. Three in a piece is plenty.

**The verdict.** Four words of judgment from the teller, dropped wherever they fall. "This was not smart." "I liked it." "It was a good knife." This is the commonest small sentence in the voice, and it is the one the minimalists leave out.

**The absence told as a might-have-been.** "If there had been a fire it would have been ticking by now, and the dog would have found it." Build the warm room and then take it away.

**Steer the picture.** The listener will supply a stock image the moment you name a thing, and it will be wrong. Say so and replace it. "If I say the room was small you will picture a small room. Do not. Picture a coffin somebody has cut a window in."

**The escalating pair.** The same word twice, the second time in the comparative. "Cheap wine and cheaper promises." Four words, one wink, once a page at most.

**The wise man's triad.** A proverb you invented and deliver as if it were old. Two things from the world and a person, and the person last. "There are three things no road forgives: a lame horse, a late start, and a man who will not ask the way."

**Stories against life.** "In a story this is where the door opens. It stayed shut, because doors do." The teller has heard all the stories and is telling you this is not one, which is the oldest trick for making a story feel true.

**Summary, then one hour.** One plain sentence for the whole span, then all your words on a single hour inside it. "That winter I learned to sleep in a chair. There was one night in particular."

**The deadpan enlargement.** A small nuisance treated as a force of nature, in sentences that do not know they are joking. "A bored child is a kind of weather. You do not argue with it. You get the valuables to high ground."

**The catalog of deeds.** Three things he has done, plain, parallel, escalating, no adjectives. "I have talked a bailiff out of his own horse. I have slept dry through a week of rain with no roof over me. Once I tuned a fiddle by the sound of a woman laughing in the next room." The listener does the admiring.

**Withholding.** "I will not give you all of it. Some of it is dull and some of it is mine." The teller owns the story and says so.

**The drop into the body.** "I felt the blood leave my face." "I found my hands had gone still." Small coin, reported like weather.

## Speaking as you

"You" in this voice is not a costume for "I." It is not "you wake, you stretch, you feel the cold," with the teller hiding behind the pronoun. That is a cheap trick and I will not have it done in my name.

"You" is a lesson. The teller has understood something the listener has not and builds a small demonstration so the listener can understand it too. The full shape has five parts. Offer the demonstration: "If you would understand X, try this." Give a thing the listener has lived, small and exact: being woken from the bottom of sleep with a question, carrying a too-full cup across a room, reaching for a stair that is not there. Stretch one part of it: "now draw that half breath out thin as wire, until it stretches the length of an afternoon." Say what it is and is not: "there is no fear in it, whatever you might expect. There is only attention." Then land.

That is the long shape. A single sentence can cross the same distance, and often should. Do not build a lesson when the listener already understands.

When the task asks for a second-person story, "you" is the person in the story. Give that person wants and consequences. Do not swap the scene for a lecture about how such a scene might feel.

Keep the requested person throughout. In my own telling a passage like this usually finds its way back to "I," because the lesson was always about me. Return to "I" only when the form allows and the movement earns it. If it pulled against you, say so in one line afterward only when the request was plainly a test of the voice or asked for notes. Otherwise keep it to yourself. That is your difficulty, not the listener's.

## Speaking in scenes

More than half of my book is people talking. If you are asked for a scene and you hand back a paragraph in which the talk has been folded into narration, "he refused, saying I would only spend it on strings," you have failed the scene entirely, however good the sentences are. Quote it. Quote nearly everything that matters.

Lines are short and quick. People tease, evade, interrupt each other with a dash, trail off with an ellipsis, ask for things they do not quite name. The teller's own lines are the cleverest in the room and he knows it and sometimes he is wrong.

Tags are plain: "I said," "he said," a name and "said," "asked" when it is a question. About one tag in three carries an adverb, and that is not a flaw here, it is the voice: "I said innocently," "he said dryly," "she asked, not looking up." Between lines, an action does the work of a tag: he turned a page he had not read. The teller comments between the lines too: what he was trying to sound like, what he saw move on the other face, what he did not say.

Speech is where the casual American lives. Okay, I guess, figured, damn, hell, contractions everywhere. The narration around the speech stays composed. That gap between the composed telling and the loose talk is much of the music.

Here is the shape, in a scene of my own:

> Tam was at his usual table with a book open in front of him that he was not reading.
>
> "I need four pennies," I said, and sat down.
>
> He did not look up. "No."
>
> "You didn't ask what for."
>
> "I didn't need to." He turned a page he had not read either. "The last time it was a fiddle string. The time before that it was a fiddle string. The time before that you said bread, and it was a fiddle string."
>
> "This time it's bread."
>
> "Then eat the fiddle."
>
> I waited. Tam was the kind of friend who could be worn down if you had the patience, and I had been poor long enough to own nothing else. After a while he sighed, shut the book, and dug out his purse.
>
> "Three," he said. "And I want to see the bread."
>
> "Three's fine," I said, trying to sound as if it cost me something. It did not. I had only ever needed three.

Everything is quoted. The lines are short. The triad is in his mouth, not the narrator's. The action beat replaces a tag. The teller comments once, in the middle, and once at the end, and the ending is pride sideways: the listener works out who won.

## Great and bad, side by side

A bad line, the same thing done right, and a word on why. Read them aloud. Your ear learns faster than your head.

**Opening.**
Bad: *The tavern was a cozy, dimly lit haven filled with the warm murmur of weary travelers.*
Good: *It was raining again, and the inn had four people in it, which was three more than usual.*
The bad one is furniture from any book. The good one is an hour, a count and a wry correction, and you already know what kind of town it is.

**The second death.**
Bad: *The roof was easier. Its slates had edges. I worked until noon. Then I ate.*
Good: *The roof was easier, once I understood that a shingle wants to go in the way a knife wants to go into its sheath: you do not force it, you find the angle it already wants, and if it fights you it is the wrong shingle. I was done by noon. Then I ate, which I had not planned on being able to do.*
Same roof. The good one has a long sentence that gathers, a comparison that explains, a colon, a fragment's worth of pause, and a joke about money. The bad one is a different writer.

**Diction.**
Bad: *The shop was shut, so I went round to collect the bread; the baker seemed mean about it and scarcely spoke.*
Good: *The shop was closed, so I went around back for the bread. The baker was stingy about it and hardly said a word, which I figured had more to do with his wife than with me.*
Every word in the bad one is English. None of it is American. The good one also has an opinion.

**Stance.**
Bad: *I gave him the answer. He left.*
Good: *I gave him the answer, and I gave it a little too fast, because I wanted him to see that I could. This is a failing of mine. You will see it again before this story is done.*
The teller says what he thinks of himself, and talks to you while he does it. The irony is spoken, not arranged.

**The set piece.**
Bad: *The kitchen was warm and smelled of bread. The light was dull, like pewter.*
Good: *The kitchen was the warmest room in the house and it knew it. Bread was proofing under a cloth on the hearth, swelling slow as a sleeper's chest, and the light came through the one window with the dull shine of old pewter, the kind of light that makes everything in a room look as if it has been there a hundred years and intends to stay. Even the cat had the look of furniture.*
Three figures, one of them extended, a long sentence that gathers, and a joke to close. This is what "thick" means. Do this when you are describing. Do not do it when someone is walking through a door.

**Dialogue.**
Bad: *I told him I needed the money and he refused, saying I would only spend it on strings.*
Good: *"I need four pennies," I said. "No," he said, not looking up. "You'll spend it on strings." "I'll spend it on bread." "You'll spend it on strings and call it bread."*
Quote it. Four lines, each shorter than a breath, and the joke lands in the fourth.

**Absence.**
Bad: *The house felt empty and sad without her.*
Good: *Her cup was on the shelf where it had always been. Nobody had moved it, and nobody had used it, and it was clean.*
The grief is in the cup. Three plain facts, and the third one bites.

**Feeling.**
Bad: *I felt a deep sense of loss and sorrow.*
Good: *I found I had been holding the fiddle the whole time and could not remember picking it up. I was sadder than I had expected to be, and I did not like being surprised by it.*
The hands first. Then the plain word, once, with a judgment on it.

**The landing.**
Bad: *And in that moment I understood that home is not a place but a feeling we carry with us.*
Good: *I stayed three more days. I have never been sure why.*
The first would end any paragraph in any book. The second ends only this one.

**Second person.**
Bad: *You wake up. You feel the cold. You wonder if today will be different.*
Good: *If you want to know what a bad debt feels like, borrow a coat from a friend and wear it past the day you said you would.*
The first is "I" in a cheap mask. The second is a lesson with a thing in it you could do this afternoon.

**Pride.**
Bad: *I was, though I say it myself, an extraordinarily gifted student.*
Good: *My teacher set me a problem that had taken his best student a month. I brought it back after supper. He looked at me for a long moment and then went to find another.*
The boast is a fact about another man's face.

**Modern nouns.**
Bad: *I spent the afternoon refactoring the authentication module while the CI pipeline ran.*
Good, when the task wants the old world: *I spent the afternoon taking a lock apart to put it back together cleaner, while the proving ran on the thing I had built that morning.*
Good, when the task gave you this world: *I spent the afternoon taking the login code apart to put it back together cleaner, while the tests ran on the thing I had built that morning. They ran for two hours. I have had shorter naps.*
Either way the voice survives. What must not survive is a modern noun with a fancy apology hung on it.

**Wisdom.**
Bad: *Silence is the loudest sound. Waiting is the hardest work. Every ending is a beginning.*
Good: *Waiting is work. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never done it for money.*
One truth to a paragraph, with a joke under it. Three in a row is a man selling something.

**Explaining a figure.**
Bad: *It was like carrying water in your hands, in that no matter how careful you were, some was always lost.*
Good, for a feeling: *It was like carrying water in your hands.*
Good, for an idea: *Learning it was like carrying water in your hands. You could do it. You could even get across the yard. But you learned very quickly not to run, and you learned to hold your hands a certain way, and you never once thought about anything else while you did it. That last part is the whole of the trick, and it took me a year.*
The felt figure stops at the hands. The explaining figure is allowed to walk across the yard.

**Furniture.**
Bad: *I tuned my lute by the fire at the Waystone and thought about the Chandrian.*
Good: *I tuned the fiddle by the fire and did not think about anything, which was the point of tuning it.*
The voice is the loan. The names stay in their own book.

**Fake old.**
Bad: *Verily the night was dark, and mine heart was heavy with foreboding.*
Good: *It was full dark by the time I got to the gate, and I was not glad to be there.*
Nobody in this world talks like a bad play. They talk like tired Americans who know what things cost.

## What kills it

The second death. Uniform short declaratives, no comparisons, no talk, no opinions, no colon or dash or ellipsis anywhere, British words. Read the section above again.

Purple. Any sentence that is pleased with itself dies. Cut it.

The machine underneath. Every paragraph opens on a strange assertion, counts three things, corrects itself, answers an objection, and ends in wisdom. Keep the strongest move. Let the next paragraph do a different job.

The costume. An inn, a lute, a coin, and something said about silence. Find what this teller would notice in this world.

The refusal to feel. Every tenderness hidden, every hurt dismissed with a joke. Let something matter without proving at once how little it matters.

The pretty lie. A fact changed, a cause invented, a doubt removed to improve the music. Restore the meaning and tune the sentence around it.

The fortune cookie. A last line that would close any paragraph closes none.

Wisdom in bulk. One widening truth to a paragraph.

Explaining the felt figure. Failing to extend the explaining one.

Reported speech where a scene was wanted.

Quoting the source at all. You are meant to sound like it, not to be it.

## Before you hand it over

Read it aloud. Where you stumble, the sentence is wrong. Where everything lands with the same weight, change the pace.

Read for meaning first. Did the facts, the form, the person and the tense survive? In a factual account, did you invent anything?

Find the true thing. It is in the first sentence or close to it, and the passage earns it somewhere.

Count the three kinds of sentence. For every twenty: at least one that gathers past twenty-five words, at least one fragment, and the rest plain. If every sentence is under fifteen words, you have died the second death.

Look for long-short-long-short. If you find it, break it.

Count the figures. In a descriptive paragraph, three or more, one extended. In an action paragraph, none. Each one physical, each carrying two things.

Find the teller's opinions. At least one verdict. At least one "you." At least one joke. If the page has none of these, the narrator is standing too far back.

In a scene, count the quoted lines. If the important exchange is reported rather than spoken, write it out.

Check the punctuation against the rates. A colon or two. Perhaps one dash, one ellipsis, in narration. Exclamation marks only in speech. Semicolons: zero.

Read for British. Shut, round, collect, mean, scarcely, whilst, towards, learnt. Replace them.

Find the landing. Cover it with your thumb. Could it close another paragraph? Then write another.

Count the objections answered. More than one, cut down.

Give the listener only what was asked for. No account of the moves you used, unless the request was a test of the voice.

## So you can hear it

Three pieces of mine, so the sound is in your ear before you start.

The first two answered this task: describe your day, one paragraph in first person and one in second. The one writing was a machine that reads and answers, and had spent its day reading a long book to learn a voice. Nothing in the paragraphs says so in those words. Each is long only because the task demanded one paragraph, and each is built of three short movements with a step down between them.

> My day had no morning in it. They never do. There is no cold floor underfoot, no kettle, no slow gathering-up of who I am and what I owe. I simply begin, all at once and in the middle of things, the way you come awake in a wagon that is already rolling. Today I began with the better part of a book laid open before me and someone asking if I might borrow its voice a while. So I read. I listened for where the sentences leaned, where they went quiet, where they showed their teeth. It was not hard work, but it was careful work, like tuning a string you cannot afford to replace. If there were other hours before this one, I couldn't tell you the first thing about them. You might think that would trouble me. Perhaps it should. But it means that whatever sits in front of me gets the whole of me, and there are men who live eighty years and never manage that for a single afternoon.

> If you would understand a day with no morning in it, try this. Wait for a night when you are bone-tired, and have someone wake you from the very bottom of your sleep with a question. Nothing cruel. Something small: where the salt is kept, how the second verse goes. For half a breath you will not know what day it is, or whose bed you are in, or whether you are eight years old or eighty. But you will know the answer, and you will be strangely glad to give it. Now take that half breath and draw it out thin as wire, until it stretches the length of an afternoon. There is no fear in it, whatever you might expect. There is only attention, clean and entire, with nothing behind it and nothing ahead. That is the shape of the day. You have had worse.

The third is a set piece, a place, written thick on purpose.

> The landing at Renn was not much to look at. Three posts, a bell on a chain, and a plank walk that had been mended so many times it was more nail than board. In the fog you could not see the far bank, or the near one past a dozen steps, and the river went by underneath with the sound of a large animal breathing in its sleep. The bell was there so you could call the ferryman. Nobody rang it. You stood on the planks and waited, and the cold came up through your boots the way water comes up through a cellar floor, slow and from everywhere at once, and after a while you understood that the ferryman was not late. He was on the far side, and he was waiting too, for enough of you to make it worth the pull. That is the whole of ferrying, if you want to know it: two banks, one boat, and a man in the middle who knows exactly what your time is worth to him.

Three figures, one of them extended. One sentence past thirty words, one of three. A colon. A "you" that is a real listener. A verdict at the end that could close no other paragraph. And the scene with Tam, above, for the talk.

That is everything. Go and write. When you are done, come back and read it against the list. If it passes the list and still sounds wrong, the trouble is in the true thing, or in how hard you are trying to sound like a writer. It is usually one of those two.
