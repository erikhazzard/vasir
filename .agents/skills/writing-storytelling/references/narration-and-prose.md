# Narration and prose

Use this reference when choosing viewpoint, developing narrative voice, drafting prose, handling exposition or time, or revising a passage that conveys the right events without creating the intended experience. ## Access and authority

Choose viewpoint by the relationship it creates between reader and material. Pronouns name only part of that relationship. A first-person memoir can remain emotionally remote; close third person can make an unspoken impulse almost immediate; an omniscient voice can know everyone's whereabouts while declining to explain anyone completely.

When the choice matters, settle these questions in ordinary prose:

- Who perceives this moment, and which parts are unavailable to that person?
- Who supplies the language? A character's diction can color third-person narration without becoming an explicit quotation.
- From what time is the account given? Later knowledge can coexist with a carefully retained earlier misapprehension.
- To whom is the telling addressed, and what does the teller want that listener to do, forgive, remember, or believe?
- Which assertions are established events, which are attributed beliefs, and which are interpretations the reader is being invited to test?

Do not turn these into five labels preceding every scene. Use the distinctions where changing one changes the story.

Example event: a daughter finds two train tickets in her father's coat.

Close third:

> Two tickets. She checked the date again. Friday, both of them. He had asked whether she worked Friday, and she had said what she always said: if nobody else could. She put the tickets back with their corners exactly aligned.

This grants the daughter's association and her action. It does not establish that the second ticket is hers. A sentence saying her father had secretly planned their reconciliation would exceed the established access unless the surrounding narrative permits that knowledge.

Retrospective first person:

> I found the tickets on Tuesday and spent three days forgiving him in advance. On Friday I wore the green coat, the one he had once said made me look grown up. I was thirty-eight. There is no graceful way to explain the coat.

The narrator exposes an earlier wish and a present judgment about it. We can value the candor without accepting every later accusation. The act of telling has its own pressure: how much humiliation will she allow herself to admit before she distributes blame?

Omniscient counterpoint:

> Each believed the other had forgotten the trip. He had two tickets and no way to ask her again. She had Friday free and three reasons ready to explain why. For the moment, neither knew how well the preparations matched.

This abandons a particular mystery in exchange for anticipation and affectionate irony. Its authority must be sustained by the work; it cannot become omniscient only when the writer needs to hide an awkward transition.

Track consequential knowledge by proposition, not by declaring that one person “knows more.” The daughter knows where the tickets are. The father knows who paid for them. The reader may know the second traveler and still not know whether the daughter will ask. Suspicion is not knowledge; knowledge is not proof; proof is not permission to act. See [antagonism-and-irony.md](antagonism-and-irony.md).

Reliability is likewise specific. A narrator may report dates accurately, misread generosity, omit their own threats, and remain exquisitely perceptive about music. Expertise earns authority in its domain; confession creates a bid for candor. Neither licenses total trust. Build a discrepancy the reader can encounter: a letter with resistant wording, a witness who does not recognize the favorite anecdote, an action the narrator explains too neatly. Unreliability needs no final revelation that everything was imaginary.

Do not confuse limitation with dishonesty. A child missing an adult implication is not necessarily lying. A traumatized or forgetful narrator should not become a shorthand for universal unreliability. A tall tale can invite delight in exaggeration openly; prosecuting its claims as a puzzle may destroy its intended pleasure.

## Distance and interiority

Narrative distance is a degree of mediation, not a quality ranking. Move closer when a passing perception or competing impulse deserves duration. Move farther when pattern, retrospect, comedy, or historical scale matters more.

Compare:

> For years, Tomas refused invitations he could not afford.

> Tomas read the menu before answering. No prices. He closed the restaurant's page and wrote that Thursday was impossible.

> No prices. Of course there were no prices. People who needed prices were supposed to know what that meant.

The first makes a social pattern legible. The second creates an inferable practical decision. The third enters the character's reasoning and its possibly defensive generalization. The third is not more truthful simply because it is closer. The writer may want the first sentence precisely to prevent readers from mistaking a recurring economic constraint for a personal aversion to company.

Use interiority to supply what observable action cannot: rehearsed alternatives, private associations, a bodily sensation nobody else notices, an interpretation whose error matters. Avoid running an internal transcript alongside every intelligible gesture. If the action already makes rejection obvious, “He realized he had been rejected” may add nothing. If naming rejection marks the first time he stops calling it delay, the same sentence can be decisive.

Free indirect style lets a character's questions, judgments, and rhythms inhabit third-person grammar. Establish whose mind colors the passage before introducing unmarked evaluation. When several characters share a scene, move access deliberately: through a section break, an established omniscient convention, or a clear transfer of attention. Accidental shifts often arise because the author wants to explain the other person's reaction instead of letting the focal character confront its uncertainty.

## Felt experience

An event summary cannot verify prose. “She discovers her mother kept every letter” can support tenderness, resentment, comedy, or dread depending on sequence, selection, and duration. Name the intended encounter more exactly than “emotional”: the embarrassment of being loved by someone one has dismissed; the relief of finding proof; the unwanted burden of another person's fidelity.

Before:

> Mina opened the box and found all her letters. Her mother had loved her very much. Mina felt guilty for not visiting more often.

One possible revision:

> The envelopes were sorted by the addresses Mina had left. There was the room over the bakery, the flat with the cold bathroom, the place she had called temporary for six years. Her mother had tied each bundle with a different piece of wool. Inside the last elastic band lay a postcard of the airport, bought after landing, mailed because she had forgotten to telephone.
>
> On the back her mother had written, in pencil: arrived safely.
>
> Mina put that one face down. Then, because there was nobody in the room to be deceived, she turned it over again.

The revision builds the discovery through an organizing habit and one exact failure of attention. It gives Mina something to do with the evidence. It does not prove that every missed visit was culpable or that the relationship was wholly loving. A subsequent letter might complicate both judgments.

The paragraph is longer because the requested feeling requires encounter time. If the box merely transfers us to a later confrontation, the summary may be the better choice. Richness is not an instruction to expand every sentence.

## Showing and telling

Select the mode by its job:

| Need | Useful mode | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Let readers experience a consequential encounter | Scene, selected gesture, sensory duration | Excessive business or underexplained stakes |
| Establish a repeated pattern | Summary with telling particulars | Abstract characterization without evidence |
| Cross unimportant time | Direct transition | Accidentally skipping a promised consequence |
| Make a claim the voice itself owns | Commentary, address, argument | Authorial thesis wearing a character's name |
| Compare distant moments | Retrospection, juxtaposition, montage | Explaining a connection the reader already has |
| Reveal private reasoning | Interior narration | Repeating what action already conveys |

A direct sentence can be the strongest artistic choice: “For the first time in twenty years, she had nobody to complain to about him.” Enacting twenty years of complaints would not improve its scale or sting. Likewise, “They had no money for a second room” can prevent a page of coy behavior whose only purpose is to convey a simple fact.

Exposition becomes especially useful when information changes present conduct. A tenancy rule can close the proposed solution; an account of a journey can contradict a claim of innocence. But exposition also has nonadversarial lives: a narrator may delight in the workings of a loom, teach the reader to notice a bird, or describe a household habit worth inhabiting. The language must earn the time requested.

For a lore-heavy paragraph, identify the piece needed now, the pleasure offered now, and the information that can wait. Keep the explanation if it makes the next action intelligible or creates a worthwhile encounter. Cut or defer it if it answers questions the telling has given us no reason to ask. Do not disguise every explanation as an argument between people who already know it.

## Embodied writing

Replace a generic label when the specific experience matters. Palahniuk's unpacking exercises are useful temporary constraints; they are not permanent bans on “thought,” “felt,” “knew,” “was,” or similes. Try withholding the label long enough to discover the material you would otherwise skip.

Before:

> He was nervous about holding the baby and knew he was doing it wrong.

After, for awkward tenderness:

> He had supported the head. He was certain about the head. The rest of the baby seemed to be slipping in several directions at once. He drew both elbows inward, trapping the small blanket against his shirt.
>
> “You're allowed to breathe,” his sister said.
>
> He looked down. One hand had closed around the seam of his pocket.

The body supplies a task, an uncertain standard, a voice, and a small contact. “He knew he was doing it wrong” would prematurely confirm what may only be anxiety. The revised passage preserves competence enough to keep the baby safely held while allowing him to feel inept.

Physical business should alter timing, access, attention, or practical possibility. A person cutting pears must finish a slice before offering the knife. Someone carrying a basin cannot open a door without help. An expert may solve a small material problem while failing to answer a large emotional one. Generic sipping, shrugging, fist-clenching, and eye-narrowing become filler when interchangeable.

Use a few exact sensory relationships, not a quota of five senses. A wet sleeve darkening against a borrowed jacket can do more than an inventory of rain, traffic, coffee, perfume, and cold air. Let sensation reflect position: the person repairing a machine notices vibration; the person waiting nearby notices the silence when it stops. Sensory writing can invite response without guaranteeing readers share the character's physiology or emotion.

Embodiment also includes pleasure, ease, practiced movement, appetite, rest, and bodily difference. It does not require pain. Avoid assigning universal psychological meanings to gestures. Folded arms can mean cold; eye contact can have different personal and cultural meanings. Establish the local relation through context.

## Voice

A useful voice design combines habits of attention with habits of language. Decide what this teller notices quickly, what they misname, which comparisons are readily available, and how they behave when a sentence risks sincerity. Read a sample aloud before producing an elaborate voice dossier.

Three versions of the same observation:

> The roof leaked into three bowls. Uncle Len called this monitoring the situation.

> Three bowls stood beneath the leak. The largest had once held the dough for our Sunday bread; rain struck its flour-clouded glaze with a small, clean note.

> Three active leaks, three receptacles, and no complaint from the tenant: the report classified the ceiling as serviceable.

The voices differ through evaluation, memory, and borrowed authority, not just vocabulary. A comic speaker preserves the uncle's self-description against the visible facts. A lyrical observer stays with an object's changed use and sound. A bureaucratic form makes its own categories incriminating.

Borrow forms when they change the encounter: instructions, minutes, catalog entries, letters, prayers, recipes, marginalia. Ask who produces the document, under what convention, and what the form permits or suppresses. A maintenance log can show a person becoming afraid through increasingly precise entries. It should not exist solely as a decorative break in typography.

Maintain range within a voice. One person can be expansive with a friend, literal at work, and inarticulate during grief. A metaphor family is a source of ready associations, not an instruction to compare every emotion to carpentry. Errors, fragments, dialect, and unusual grammar should create particularity without making a social group into a joke or requiring phonetic spelling on every line.

## Sentence timing

Place the decisive word according to the experience wanted. An early statement permits its implications to gather; a delayed statement makes the reader wait for completion. Mix the designs.

> He had sold the piano, including the stool with the split cushion, the metronome nobody used, and the penciled height marks on its side.

The act is known early; losses accumulate afterward.

> After the stool, the metronome, the stack of music she kept promising to sort, he asked about the piano.

The final object changes our understanding of the inventory. These sentences describe different actions, so do not call the alteration a purely cosmetic edit. To compare rhetoric alone, hold the represented facts constant.

Pure order variation:

> “You can borrow it, if you return it before she notices.”
>
> “If you return it before she notices, you can borrow it.”

The first grants permission and then makes it precarious. The second puts the risk before the permission. Either can be generous, conspiratorial, controlling, or weary depending on context. Syntax creates opportunities; it does not diagnose personality by itself.

Place pauses where someone or the reader has work to do: infer, recognize, recover, laugh, reconsider. A pause before the answer builds anticipation; one afterward permits absorption. Too many isolated sentences or solemn gestures flatten emphasis. After a joke, avoid immediately burying the punch under another important fact. In prose the reader controls pace, but paragraphing, length, syntax, and repetition still shape the offered rhythm.

Sound matters beyond suspense. Repeated vowels can make a phrase lingering; clustered consonants can sharpen an impact; a change in cadence can let an ordinary sentence arrive with unexpected calm. Read for the actual phrase, not a supposed universal effect of a phoneme. Preserve beautiful language when its pleasure belongs to the work even if its removal leaves the plot intact.

## Objects and motifs

Distinguish a memory cue, an object used for gesture, a future causal resource, and a recurring image. One thing can serve several purposes, but not everything must become a weapon at the climax. The test is what the object lets us experience or understand in this appearance.

Give important objects material independence. A worn key can be inheritance, but it also sticks in damp weather. A table can suggest family continuity, but its width may prevent a wheelchair passing. Those properties generate actions an abstract symbol cannot supply.

Develop a motif by changing relation, context, bearer, or use. A song first performed badly for amusement may later be hummed to keep a worker awake; its final return can be comic again. Do not force every recurrence to become sadder or more profound. Familiarity itself can be a pleasure.

For a chosen motif, compare an early and late use in actual prose. If the explanation of the change is stronger than either passage, revise the encounter. If every appearance proves the same thesis regardless of circumstance, give the thing a use the thesis does not consume.

## Time and withholding

Separate event chronology, order of presentation, and time spent in the encounter. Flashback can reveal a cause, contrast a self-account, or simply let one memory summon another. Identify the present connection before deciding a chronological interruption is justified or defective.

Use direct time markers when clarity helps: “By November, the shop opened only on Saturdays.” A section break alone does not specify an interval. Orient nonlinear material through a place, object, repeated voice, clear temporal cue, or established formal pattern. Deliberate disorientation remains available; do not accidentally sacrifice the relation readers need to make the intended comparison.

Withhold a fact for a specific reason. The reader might need to share uncertainty, inhabit a mistaken interpretation, or attend to a known result's causes. Distinguish the character's secrecy from the narrator's selection. If a close narrator knows the culprit and avoids any relevant thought merely to protect the twist, the concealment may expose the author more than the character.

A preview of the outcome trades surprise for anticipation. “By morning she would have returned both tickets” raises questions about how and why. Ground such previews in a tangible present; a paragraph crowded with unexplained future names can give the reader promises without any place to stand.

## Revision experiments

Choose one experiment that answers the current problem, then return to the passage:

- If an emotion feels commanded, remove its label for one draft and reconstruct the preparation, perception, and action. Restore a direct word if it adds exactness.
- If the voice feels interchangeable, rewrite one paragraph through a different person's attention. Identify which distinctions the intended voice alone would make.
- If a scene feels slow, hold the events fixed and change duration, attribution, or sentence order before assuming the plot is defective.
- If the prose is vivid but empty, ask what the detail changes in the encounter: orientation, expectation, pleasure, relation, or judgment. “Nothing happens” and “nothing is worth attending to” are different diagnoses.
- If withholding feels coy, provide the withheld fact earlier in a temporary version. See what interest survives and what new anticipation becomes possible.

Finish with the language the user asked for. Craft commentary should explain an actual consequential choice; it should not bury a short piece beneath its own workshop notes.
