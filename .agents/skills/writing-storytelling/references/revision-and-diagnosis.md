# Revision and diagnosis

Use this reference when a draft is not producing its intended effect, the user requests critique or revision, feedback conflicts, or changes have introduced continuity problems. Diagnose before mutating. A review request authorizes an evidence-backed diagnosis; a revision request authorizes the scoped edit. Route specific repairs to the relevant craft reference after locating the problem.

## Begin with the intended encounter

Establish what the work wants to do before deciding it has failed. Use the user's request, the draft's actual promises, and any stated preferences. If intent is uncertain, give the strongest plausible reading and mark the assumption; do not demand a complete artistic manifesto before making a useful local diagnosis.

Distinguish a craft problem from a preference. A speaker becoming impossible to identify is a clarity problem. A slow paragraph may be a problem in a chase and a pleasure in an account of watching bread rise. An unresolved motive can be the center of a portrait; an unresolved route can break the solution to a locked-room puzzle. The same feature changes significance with the work's invitation.

Read once for the encounter before annotating every mechanism when the scope permits. Record what holds attention, what is felt, where anticipation shifts, and where footing is lost. Preserve actual strengths explicitly: the kitchen's warmth, the narrator's abrasive charm, the pause after the daughter's refusal, the pleasure of competence. A repair that destroys the reason to read the work is not complete.

Do not measure the work against every reference in this skill. Select the smallest relevant lens. A lyrical paragraph does not need a subplot audit; a causal climax may need more than line editing.

## Separate observation from explanation

A useful note identifies the passage, the response or inconsistency, the likely mechanism, and a next action that could test or repair it. Keep confidence proportionate.

Weak note:

> The protagonist lacks agency. Make her more active.

Useful note:

> In the three chapters after the dismissal, Ada receives plans from other people and follows each without choosing among alternatives. The final decision to lead the strike therefore introduces an initiative the earlier sequence has not let us experience. Either establish an earlier consequential choice, or make the late initiative a deliberate first act and give the turn enough pressure and duration.

The second note names an actual pattern and allows more than one artistic solution. “Agency” becomes a question about available choice and consequence, not an instruction to add motion or dominance.

Keep four claims distinct when they diverge:

- Textual observation: the supervisor gives Ada the keys.
- Interpretation: the gift creates trust, transfers labor, recruits complicity, or several at once.
- Reader response: a particular reader felt relief, suspicion, indifference, or confusion.
- Authorial intention: the writer wanted the gift to enact forgiveness.

The intention does not establish the interpretation, and one reader's response does not establish a universal effect. If the text supports multiple readings deliberately, preserve that condition rather than treating ambiguity as an automatic defect.

## Locate the cause

The visible symptom often appears downstream from the missing cause. An unearned climax may need earlier preparation, not a stronger final speech. A slow middle may repeat the same negotiation, not merely use long sentences. A cruel-seeming character may have a constrained choice the reader cannot yet see.

| Symptom | First questions | Suitable next reference or tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ending feels arbitrary | What action produces it? What preparation makes that action available? | [plot-and-structure.md](plot-and-structure.md); dependency sketch |
| Ending feels preachy | Do events support the stated cause? Does the opposing good remain real? | [controlling-idea.md](controlling-idea.md) |
| Character feels schematic | Does conduct exceed assigned traits? Are motives specific and situational? | [characters.md](characters.md); an enacted scene |
| Transformation is unconvincing | Could the old motive explain the final act equally well? | [character-arcs.md](character-arcs.md); contrastive test |
| Dialogue feels written | Are responses addressed to each other? Are intentions and constraints particular? | [dialogue.md](dialogue.md); read aloud |
| Scene feels inert | Does a promised consequential encounter occur? Or is another experience intended and insufficiently exact? | [scenes-and-beats.md](scenes-and-beats.md); [genre-and-form.md](genre-and-form.md) |
| Mystery feels unfair | Was the evidence available? Does the conclusion depend on an unstated fact? | Proposition-specific knowledge map |
| World feels generic | Which condition changes what a person can do or enjoy? | [world-and-myth.md](world-and-myth.md) |
| Prose feels emotionally flat | Is the feeling prepared and encountered, or merely named? | [narration-and-prose.md](narration-and-prose.md) |
| Middle feels repetitive | Do tactics, relationships, costs, or live questions actually change? | A short event inventory |

These are starting questions, not automated verdicts. A scene can sustain atmosphere without a value reversal; a stable protagonist can remain compelling without a secret flaw; a successful good act can remain morally mixed. Diagnose the particular work.

## Match the test to the uncertainty

Use a representation capable of containing the problem. An outline can test whether a cause precedes its effect. It cannot establish whether a voice is funny. A polished scene can test a relationship's plausibility while concealing that the full investigation has no solution.

Use a chronology for travel, age, sequence, and duration. Use a minimal information map for consequential differences in knowledge or proof. Use a cast map when relational functions overlap or disappear. Use an event inventory to distinguish progression from repetition. Use actual prose or rehearsal when rhythm, charm, silence, implication, or performance is the uncertainty.

Do not treat any map as neutral measurement. A value label, scene boundary, or judgment of intensity is an interpretation. Record the concrete event beside the label when disagreement matters. A neutral cell may mean the passage prepares later action, deepens perception, supplies pleasure, or simply does not do the thing this map measures.

A prototype should make one discriminating question answerable. If the concern is whether a reunion feels earned, draft the reunion with enough prior context to understand the offer and cost. If the concern is whether a late clue can be inferred, present the relevant evidence without explaining the intended solution. A tiny extract cannot reproduce every accumulated effect; report what the test can and cannot establish.

Do not use spreadsheets, beat counts, polarity scores, or model-generated ratings as certification of literary quality. They can reveal a pattern worth investigating. They cannot replace the encounter or prove a universal response.

## Contrastive and counterfactual tests

Use alternatives to reveal what actually carries the intended effect. Keep the changed variable narrow enough to learn from the comparison. These are editorial thought experiments and draft experiments, not controlled scientific evidence.

**Competing explanation.** If a character returns stolen money and the intended reading is honesty, ask whether fear of discovery explains the act equally well. If so, the arc may remain ambiguous. The repair could be a later opportunity to blame an innocent person that the character declines despite a real advantage. It could also be keeping mixed motives and revising the outline's claim from “becomes honest” to “begins making restitution.” Do not add a gratuitous ordeal simply to prove virtue.

**Removal.** Temporarily remove a preparatory passage. What later inference, feeling, orientation, or pleasure becomes unavailable? A brief account of a ferry's last departure may be necessary to understand the character's choice to stay. The full timetable may not be. A garden passage may be essential because it makes the place worth missing, even if no logistical inference depends on it.

**Earlier disclosure.** Reveal a withheld fact sooner in a temporary version. If interest improves, the work may need anticipation rather than surprise. If shared seduction is central to the horror, earlier disclosure may destroy that particular effect while creating dread. State the tradeoff; do not assume more secrecy is more suspense.

**Available alternative.** Give the protagonist the obvious solution a reader would propose. Does the dilemma disappear? If so, determine whether the alternative is genuinely unavailable, costly in a way the work has omitted, or a better story choice. “She would never ask for help” requires a supported relation to this help, not a personality label attached to every circumstance.

**Condition change.** Hold the preferred conduct fixed and alter one enabling condition. If candor works only because the listener has independent income, the story's freedom claim depends partly on that material fact. The point is to make the claim exact, not require every novel to model every social possibility.

**Order or duration.** Keep events constant while reordering the presentation or changing the amount of page time. If a pause makes a joke land, a plot rewrite is unnecessary. If earlier explanation removes the reader's chance to recognize the clue, delay the interpretation rather than inventing a new clue.

Use only the test that bears on the live question. Running all six on every scene makes revision bureaucratic and can reward the story for satisfying the apparatus rather than its audience.

## Causal and rhetorical repairs

A causal repair changes what happens, what makes it possible, or what follows. A rhetorical repair changes how the same material becomes available: selection, order, viewpoint, wording, emphasis, or duration. Many repairs do both. Name the difference so a “line edit” does not silently change agency or culpability.

Original:

> She was brave. She opened the door even though she was terrified.

Rhetorical revision, preserving the known decision:

> The handle clicked against her ring. She waited until she could hold it still, then opened the door.

This makes bodily control and duration available, but removes explicit knowledge of fear. It may be subtler or merely less clear. If the reader needs to know the fear is real, a direct interior sentence can be appropriate.

Causal revision:

> She had nearly reached the stairs when she heard him trying the latch from the other side. She went back and opened the door.

Now a new sound prompts a return. It changes the event and may change why she acts. Do not praise the second as improved prose without acknowledging the altered story.

For minimal repair, ask which missing relation must become available. Sometimes one line suffices. If the scene's whole arrangement forces people to behave implausibly, a larger rewrite is warranted. “Smallest” means smallest change that addresses the cause, not smallest number of edited words.

Preserve deliberate tonal and formal choices. Do not convert a terse narrator into therapeutic self-explanation to prove emotional intelligence. Do not add quips to every tense scene for “voice.” Do not replace a quiet acceptance with an argument solely to supply conflict. The repair must fit the chosen work.

## Dependencies

After a material change, inspect what it previously enabled and what depended on someone knowing it. Follow dependencies forward into action and backward into preparation. File boundaries and chapter boundaries do not contain consequences.

Example: an earlier draft has a brother deliberately sell a family instrument. Revision changes the event: it was mistakenly collected with donated furniture, and he concealed the mistake.

Old confrontation:

> “You put a price on the only thing she left me.”
>
> “I needed the money.”

Those lines no longer have grounds. The injury now includes negligence, concealment, and lost opportunities for recovery, not a deliberate sale for profit.

Possible revised exchange:

> “The van was still outside when you noticed?”
>
> “I wasn't sure it was hers.”
>
> “You could have called me.”
>
> “You wouldn't have answered.”

The final line can be a plausible fear, a familiar accusation, or an excuse. Earlier scenes should supply the relationship from which readers can judge it. The repair changes the accusation without making the brother innocent by fiat.

Check affected elements: who saw the collection; whether payment exists; why a sibling took sides; what clues now mean; what restitution is possible; what the ending claims. Preserve unaffected language. Reopen distant scenes only where a dependency actually changed.

Late inventions can be prepared backward. If the ending needs a skill, prop, promise, or route, establish it earlier through an encounter with its own interest. A plant that exists only to announce its eventual use feels efficient but vacant. A character using a skill in ordinary work can establish both capacity and life beyond the climax.

Finally, read the surviving text without supplying cut material from memory. Replies to deleted lines, pronouns without antecedents, emotional responses to removed disclosures, and setup for abandoned payoffs often survive revision. Bird's account of reconnecting characters after cuts is especially useful here.

## Feedback

Ask questions about the encounter before inviting solutions. “When did you suspect the sister?” is more discriminating than “Was the twist good?” “What did you think she had agreed to?” locates a missing implication. “Where did you want to linger?” protects a pleasure that a pacing pass might erase.

Separate the reader's observation from their proposed fix. “I lost track when the uncle arrived” can be valuable even if “remove the uncle” is wrong. Compare plausible causes: too many similar names, unclear blocking, missing relationship information, or an intentional shift in access that lacks a signal.

Different readers answer different questions. A domain expert can check a procedure; a newcomer can reveal unexplained shorthand. A returning reader can inspect a payoff's preparation but cannot fully reproduce a first encounter with the surprise. A performance tests audible phrasing and timing; it does not prove that a silent page has the same effect.

Protect room for disagreement. A reader can understand and reject a character's reasoning. Disliking a person is not proof that attachment failed if the work invites fascination, dread, comic distance, or judgment. Conversely, an author's statement that discomfort is intentional does not establish that the discomfort has artistic purpose.

Model-generated criticism can suggest hypotheses, compare versions, or identify textual inconsistencies. It is not a substitute for actual audience response, lived expertise, or verification of real-world claims. Do not report imagined reader reactions as observed results.

## Revision sequence

Choose a sequence based on dependency and uncertainty, not a universal outline-first doctrine. A useful default for a located problem is:

1. State the intended effect and what the available text currently does.
2. Locate the likely cause and its scope.
3. Choose a concrete repair or discriminating alternative.
4. Make the authorized change, preserving the work's strengths.
5. Repair affected dependencies and reread the result as an encounter.

For a sprawling causal draft, examine major events before polishing scenes likely to disappear. For a voice-led work, sentence-level invention may discover the structure; it is not necessarily premature polish. McKee's extensive outlining and Coyne's restrained preplanning are different process choices. Use the one that helps this uncertainty while keeping the work revisable.

Avoid endlessly producing diagnostic artifacts after a concrete revision becomes possible. A map earns its cost by improving the next decision. Once it has done that, return to the writing.

## Worked diagnosis

Request: “The ending of this quiet family story feels unearned. Make it land without making everyone explain themselves.”

Available setup: Ivo always serves his mother's soup in the same blue bowl. After her death, his sister Dara has asked him not to save her place at every meal. He has refused. At the ending, she arrives unexpectedly.

Draft:

> Ivo looked at Dara and realized he had to move on. He put away the blue bowl. At last he understood that family meant the people who were still there.

Diagnosis: the paragraph states a thematic conversion and uses the bowl to certify it. It does not enact an encounter with Dara. It also risks equating continued remembrance with failure to value the living, a stronger claim than the setup requires. The smallest warranted repair is to give their existing difference a practical, shared action and leave the future open.

Revised passage:

> Dara stood beside the chair while Ivo finished pouring. He had put the blue bowl at its old place.
>
> “Is there enough?” she asked.
>
> He looked into the pot. There was always enough. That had been the trouble these last six Sundays: enough, and nobody coming late.
>
> He lifted the blue bowl and set it in front of her.
>
> “Mind the crack.”
>
> “I know where it is.”
>
> She turned the bowl a little. He sat down before she tasted it.

What changed: Ivo does not discard the object or abolish grief. He changes its use in response to an actual arrival. Dara's knowledge gives her a history with it independent of his permission. Sitting before she tastes the soup can release his habitual waiting, if that habit has been established. The direct sentence about enough soup gives scale to repeated absence without making either person announce a lesson.

What remains uncertain: whether the gesture signals one hospitable evening or sustained change. The story can end there if a first opening is its promise. If the intended claim is durable reconciliation, the prior relationship and later consequences need more evidence. Do not label this ending a completed cure.

Contrastive check: if Ivo moved the bowl before Dara arrived because he wanted more table space, the practical act would no longer carry the same relational response. If Dara silently accepted it with no shared knowledge, she could become an instrument of his arc. The exchange about the crack gives her something of her own without adding a speech.

Preserve the user's requested quietness. A confrontation over the bowl is an available different scene, but the evidence does not require it here.

## Stopping and handoff

Stop when the significant live problems have been addressed at the authorized scope, affected dependencies hold, and further diagnosis is no longer producing changes worth making. This is not a claim of perfection. A difficult ending can remain difficult; an opaque person can remain partly unknown; a formally strange sentence can remain strange.

For a critique, return the few highest-impact findings with textual evidence, their likely causes, and concrete repair directions. For a revision, return the revised artifact and a concise account of material changes. Report what was actually checked and what remains untested. Do not call a table's internal consistency a successful audience test.

When moving beyond fiction into memoir, factual narrative, or purpose-driven persuasion, separate artistic effectiveness from factual support and external outcomes. A coherent invented result cannot prove a product, policy, or behavior works in life. Storynomics's measurement discussion concerns a separate task; its illustrative metrics do not make reader engagement equivalent to demonstrated causation. Keep the craft claim within the evidence the work provides.
