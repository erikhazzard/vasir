# Dialogue: people acting and responding through words

Use this reference to write or revise an exchange, distinguish voices, create subtext, handle exposition, or shape interior and narratorial speech. If asked to write dialogue, deliver dialogue; do not replace the requested scene with a craft lecture. Use [scenes-and-beats.md](scenes-and-beats.md) when the encounter's turn is unclear and [narration-and-prose.md](narration-and-prose.md) when perspective or presentation carries the main problem.

## Find the actual addressee

McKee's broad dialogue model includes a character speaking to another person, to themselves, or to a reader or audience. These modes require different knowledge and responses. A woman saying “I did what I could” to her daughter may request forgiveness; saying it to herself may rehearse an account she cannot sustain; saying it to readers may shape how they judge the daughter. Do not infer the intended recipient from quotation marks alone. An exchange can be staged for a silent witness.

Distinguish speech inside an unfolding scene from a character's retrospective account of it. Both can act on a listener. The narrator may know the outcome while the person in the scene does not. An impersonal or third-person narrating presence is not automatically a character with a concealed agenda; route that case through the narration reference.

## Build the exchange from desire and response

Before writing a difficult exchange, identify what each participant immediately wants and what they are trying to preserve while seeking it. These foreground and background desires generate the shape of the approach. A parent wants an answer and wants the child to keep confiding; a trainee wants permission to act and a usable recommendation; a friend wants help without turning affection into an obligation.

For consequential turns, distinguish:

> Intended action → what the recipient takes it to mean → actual response and resulting condition.

“You can always come home” can offer security, be received as denial of adulthood, and result in less willingness to seek help. The divergence does not require the speaker secretly to intend humiliation. Annotating every part as “belittling” would erase the conflict you need to write.

Action verbs can help when dialogue is inert: reassure, invite, clarify, bargain, correct, confess, promise, forbid, acknowledge, distract, accuse, concede, joke, comfort. Use them to identify a meaningful difference, not to assign a combat tactic to every syllable. People also think together, teach, enjoy shared memories, make observations, and stay in company. McKee's own minimal-conflict case demonstrates that quiet disclosure can matter without a duel of manipulations.

## Handle subtext without compulsory concealment

McKee distinguishes the **said**, what the words articulate; the **unsaid**, what a person consciously withholds from this listener; and the **unsayable**, pressures they do not recognize well enough to articulate. Use these as questions about access and awareness. They are not three mandatory secrets behind every line.

The unsaid can become said to someone else. An unrecognized pressure may become intelligible later. A private thought need not be truer than a costly conscious commitment. When inferring motives from supplied text, separate evidence from a possible interpretation; unexplained behavior does not prove the most dramatic hidden cause.

Candor can retain subtext because speaking truth does something. “I'm frightened” may ask for a promise, permit companionship, explain a refusal, or put an emotional burden on the listener. The truth of the feeling does not settle what response is being requested.

> “I'm frightened you'll leave.”
>
> “I can stay tonight.”
>
> “That isn't what I asked.”
>
> “It's what I can promise.”

The scene is direct about fear and exact about the boundary of commitment. Replacing it with an argument about cold tea might hide the emotion while making the relationship less clear. Use indirection when it expresses actual restraint, risk, tact, uncertainty, or habit. Let directness become a consequential event when a relationship can no longer sustain its previous silence.

Subtext may also be affection both people understand, a private joke, an acknowledged ritual, or an attempt to find words for something new. It need not mean the opposite of the sentence. A character can mean “thank you” and still reveal pride through how difficult it is to say.

## Give each person a way of speaking

Design voice as a pattern of attention and adjustment. Select a few features that affect actual wording:

- What they notice first: causes, costs, exceptions, feelings, status, physical objects, practical sequence, possibility.
- What they count as evidence: witnessed events, precedent, numbers, intuition, reputation, embodied knowledge.
- Their default argument tactic: defining terms, flattering, offering a bargain, reframing, teasing, citing duty, withdrawing, asking a literal question.
- Their ordinary sentence movement: accumulate, qualify, correct, leap, balance, defer the point, begin with the consequence.
- Their metaphor resources: occupation, place, hobby, religion, family language, aspiration. Use these lightly; a gardener need not mention roots in every sentence.
- Their limits and variations: what changes with a superior, intimate, stranger, child, adversary, or unexpected kindness.

Nera tends to correct the category before answering the demand. Iven begins with what must happen next. Jo asks the practical question a polite abstraction has avoided. These are more useful defaults than assigning Nera long sentences, Iven short ones, and Jo slang.

The same speaker can retain a pattern while changing register:

> Nera to a colleague: “It's a copy of the claim, not a second claim.”
>
> Nera to a resident: “You aren't asking twice. They've counted you twice.”
>
> Nera to Jo: “You may be right. Let me find the part that annoys me.”

All three attend to distinctions, but the relationship changes technical density and intimacy. Voice should permit moods and growth. A default tactic is the first reach, not the only possible action.

A voice test can remove names from several substantial lines and ask what distinguishes the speakers. Do not demand that “Yes” uniquely identify a person. If only vocabulary differentiates them, revise attention, evidence, and response patterns. Avoid treating dialect spelling or broad cultural stereotypes as a substitute for observation and individual design.

## Write turns that answer what happened

Characters have separate minds. One may reject another's metaphor, answer the implication instead of the literal question, miss an intended joke, return to unfinished business, or notice that the request changed. Avoid alternating polished speeches that never need the previous response.

A dramatic beat is an action and a response, not necessarily one speaking turn. Several lines can sustain one attempt; a repeated sentence can become a different action after the conditions change. “Stay” said before a departure and after an admission need not carry the same request. If you label a new tactic, identify what new response or possibility it attempts.

When drafting, let each response create pressure or opportunity for the next. The writer can know the destination while allowing participants to resist the most convenient route. Do not make a person abandon their objection solely because the protagonist has supplied a quotable line.

## Worked scene: character, speech, and plot together

Original context: floodwater threatens an archive. Nera wants ferry space for crates containing residents' claims and private petitions. Iven needs to evacuate passengers. Jo knows the files and wants permission to begin work without waiting for Nera. The practical turn is from moving complete crates to preparing selected evidence that evacuees can carry. Nera's control remains only partly revised.

Weak version:

> “The archive is important,” Nera said. “We must save our history.”
>
> “People are more important than paper,” Iven said.
>
> “You must learn to trust me,” Jo said.
>
> Nera realized they were right.

The lines announce competing themes but produce no credible plan or changed permission. Revised scene:

> Iven had chalked nine circles on the loading board. He crossed one out as a woman climbed aboard carrying a sleeping boy.
>
> “Nine crates,” Nera said.
>
> “Eight places.”
>
> “The crates aren't passengers.”
>
> “The deck doesn't know that.”
>
> Jo put the smallest crate on the scale. Its needle passed the red mark.
>
> Nera lifted the lid. “These are the marsh claims. Without them, half the people you're carrying can't prove where to return.”
>
> Iven looked from the crate to the queue. “How many?”
>
> “Forty-three households.”
>
> “Not households. Pages.”
>
> Jo was already turning the files. “Two for each ordinary claim. More for the disputes.”
>
> “The petitions stay closed,” Nera said.
>
> “I know which are petitions.” Jo held out a hand. “The storeroom key.”
>
> Behind Iven, someone began lifting a trunk off the gangway. He put his hand on it. “Leave that. It's hers.”
>
> Nera took the key from her neck. “Bring me the extracts before they go aboard.”
>
> Jo did not take it. “All of them?”
>
> “Anything you aren't certain of.”
>
> “And the others?”
>
> The boy woke. The woman on the deck shifted him higher, watching the trunk.
>
> “Put them in page order. I'll come to you,” Nera said, and put the key into Jo's palm.
>
> Iven wrote across the crate's lid: CLAIMS WITH OWNERS.

The archive's significance becomes information Iven uses to change the loading plan. His response asks for a different unit, making a solution possible without abandoning passengers. Jo's expertise is demonstrated by distinguishing routine claims from disputes; Nera's privacy concern remains legitimate. The key changes access, but Nera's promise to check the work keeps certification under her control. This is shared labor, not yet delegated authority; the later arc must confront that limit. Iven's inscription makes the new arrangement concrete without removing another passenger place.

This is one event described through three lenses: Nera's philosophy shapes her request; dialogue changes the available method; the method changes what the next plot event can accomplish. The trunk and waking child give Iven another responsibility in the scene, so he remains more than an obstacle waiting to be persuaded.

## Revise wording and turn cues

Find the word or phrase at which a listener can understand enough to react. If an important reaction is held until long after that point, either shorten the line, move the revealing phrase, or let the interruption occur. Preserve delay when the speaker is intentionally controlling the floor or struggling to reach the point.

Weak cueing:

> “I gave Pell the key, because the situation was becoming difficult and I felt there was no reasonable alternative after considering what everyone had said.”
>
> “You gave him the key?”

The listener would likely react at “Pell” or “key.” Options:

> “Pell has the key.”
>
> “You gave it to him?”

Or, if evasion and interruption are the point:

> “After considering the situation, I thought the responsible—”
>
> “Who has the key?”

Specific nouns and verbs improve meaning when the speaker would use them. Do not mechanically make everyone concise. Qualification may protect someone, expose uncertainty, hold attention, or invite interruption. Ask what the extra words perform. Cut words that only repeat an already complete effect; retain repetition that changes rhythm, pressure, acknowledgment, or pleasure.

## Make silence and physical activity specific

Silence changes who must continue, wait, explain, withdraw, or risk the next request. Its meaning depends on power, knowledge, and cost. Someone may pause to resist, understand, grieve, protect a fact, or find breath. Do not label all pauses evasive. An omitted response in narrated summary is not evidence of a staged deliberate silence.

A physical task matters when it changes attention, timing, options, or interpretation. Handling files gives Jo an independent activity and evidence for a reply. An offered key changes authority. Random sipping and shrugging between every line merely interrupt rhythm.

Objects can let people address a relationship indirectly, but retain their practical reality. Returning a key can surrender access; it can also mean someone needs a spare cut. Establish the context that makes the intended meaning available. Avoid explaining a loaded gesture immediately after the reader can infer it.

## Use exposition in a present encounter

Ask why this person says this information to this listener now. The answer can be instruction, reassurance, negotiation, correction, ceremony, storytelling, or an honest requested explanation. Information need not always be weaponized. A good-faith answer can be engrossing when the question, expertise, or consequence matters.

Weak: two lifelong residents recite the town's flood history for the reader. Stronger: Iven needs the height at which the old landing becomes unusable; Nera remembers the record contradicting his estimate. Their disagreement gives the information a present use. A different scene could let an eager child request the history and enjoy the telling without inventing hostility.

Supply enough knowledge to interpret the next action. Withholding every noun in the name of mystery makes speech opaque. Conversely, do not answer every implied question before a listener needs the answer. Use summary when the exact wording is less important than the result: “He explained the copying procedure twice; the third time she asked who would be responsible for an error.”

## Write inward and narratorial dialogue

For self-talk, identify the work of the thought: rehearsing a conversation, revising a memory, granting permission, seeking courage, enjoying an imagined future, or trying to make incompatible wishes coexist. Thought can respond to sensory interruption or to its own previous sentence. It need not become two labeled debaters.

Weak interior explanation: “Nera was controlling because she feared being unnecessary.”

More particular interior movement:

> Jo could manage the ordinary files. Naturally. That was what training was for. Nera reached for the first completed extract, then found Jo's neat initials where hers should have been. Where hers had always been.

The self-correction changes what the assertion can sustain. Avoid following it with a paragraph that translates every implication. Another narrator may deliberately analyze themselves at length; judge the achieved voice rather than banning explicit thought.

For narratorial speech, distinguish the person who experienced the event from the person telling it. What do they now know, want readers to notice, or refuse to settle? A narrator may be reliable about documents and unreliable about relationships. Do not make every first-person speaker secretly deceptive. Candor, expertise, generosity, play, and retrospective uncertainty can all sustain a telling.

## Calibrate medium and register

In prose, narration can summarize, qualify, expose private thought, or contradict a speaker. On stage, timing, bodies, space, and an audience's continuous attention carry much of that work. Screen dialogue shares information with framing, performance, editing, and sound. Audio needs sufficient audible orientation. In games, repeated encounters and selectable responses must preserve what the character and player can actually know; use [interactive-and-adaptation.md](interactive-and-adaptation.md).

Read performed dialogue aloud when rhythm is uncertain. Look for breaths, interruptions, playable actions, and facts an actor or audience cannot recover. Do not solve missing evidence with excessive parentheticals. Preserve the user's chosen level of stylization, period, genre, profanity, and formality. A heightened comic voice or lyrical monologue need not mimic everyday conversation to feel exact within its work.

## Diagnose and deliver

When revising, fix the encounter before polishing isolated lines. If every speaker wants a compatible result and nothing makes their process interesting, sharper insults may only decorate emptiness. If the scene already turns well, do not redesign the plot merely to distinguish two voices.

| Symptom | Likely question | Useful repair |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone sounds alike. | Do they notice, infer, and argue differently? | Change the pattern of response before adding verbal trademarks. |
| Speech sounds like a theme essay. | What practical result does the speaker seek here? | Give the listener a consequential answer the thesis did not anticipate. |
| Subtext is unreadable. | What setup makes the implication available? | Restore an orienting fact or established relational behavior. |
| Honest dialogue feels flat. | Does the disclosure risk or request anything? | Make the response alter a promise, boundary, or permission. |
| Endless argument produces no movement. | Has any reply changed the available options? | Change knowledge, audience, cost, authority, or willingness to continue. |
| Banter erases emotion. | Is humor shared, protective, or cruel here? | Let the listener answer differently when the usual joke no longer fits. |

Deliver a complete playable passage at the requested length. If explanation is useful, briefly identify the consequential change and one or two voice decisions. Keep beat labels and hidden motives in working notes unless the user asked for annotated dialogue.
