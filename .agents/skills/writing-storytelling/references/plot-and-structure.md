## Establish the pursuit

A useful dramatic spine names a particular protagonist pursuing a particular object of desire under opposition. “A woman discovers who she is” rarely gives enough direction for plotting. “A dismissed conservator tries to recover the mural she believes she destroyed before the building is demolished” does: the pursuit can succeed, fail, change object, or reveal a wrong assumption.

Establish the following before expanding into many scenes:

- What does the protagonist want to secure or accomplish in the world of the story?
- Why does it matter to this person now?
- What value does the desired object appear to promise?
- What does the protagonist believe will work, and why is that belief plausible?
- What can actively resist, contradict, redirect, or make the pursuit costly?
- What result would unmistakably settle the governing undertaking?

Keep **desire**, **method**, **stakes**, and **meaning** separate. A musician wants a commission, pursues it by concealing a collaborator's contribution, risks her reputation, and may eventually learn that recognition purchased through erasure destroys the belonging she sought. Calling all four “ambition” makes the plot difficult to develop.

The object can be continued protection, access, reconciliation, escape, knowledge, a public act, or preservation of something already held. It need not be a physical prize. It does need enough specificity to organize attempts. A preservation story can make continuity a hard-won result.

Identify the actual center of agency in an ensemble. Several viewpoint characters do not automatically constitute a collective protagonist. A shared pursuit can be carried by people with different motives; separate pursuits can intersect without merging into one. Name the arrangement the work uses so that its climax does not promise a convergence the story cannot deliver.

## Make the inciting incident organize desire

The inciting incident changes the balance and makes a consequential pursuit necessary, attractive, or newly possible. It can be disaster, opportunity, arrival, discovery, accident, invitation, or another person's decision. The protagonist need not cause it. Their response establishes what kind of story follows.

Distinguish the event from its recognition. A departure may occur on Monday, be discovered on Thursday, and acquire a different meaning when a letter arrives on Friday. Each can cause a separate turn. Decide which one organizes the central pursuit rather than using “inciting incident” for whichever event occurs first.

An effective disturbance often does several jobs at once: interrupts an existing activity, puts a valued condition at stake, exposes a limitation in the current approach, and creates an anticipated encounter. A missing child promises a consequential confrontation with what happened to them. A marriage proposal may promise a decision about a life already being shared.

Place the disturbance when the audience can experience its force. A first-page catastrophe can work if the telling gives immediate human access; a later disturbance can work if preceding scenes already sustain a compelling undertaking. Do not fill the opening with neutral biography to reach an arbitrary percentage.

The response may be denial or refusal, provided it causes something. Refusing an assignment can transfer it to an unprepared colleague; refusing a farewell can leave a relationship defined by the last argument. Passive delay that leaves every condition unchanged needs another source of present interest.

## Build a causal progression

Use this sequence to interrogate an outline, not as prose to display mechanically:

**Present condition → desired next result → chosen action → expected response → actual response → changed condition → next consequential choice.**

The crucial connection is from the actual response to the next choice. “She fails, then visits another place” may merely move the itinerary. “She fails because the witness distrusts officials; her unauthorized visit now endangers the witness's work, so she must protect that work before asking again” creates a changed undertaking.

For each major transition, distinguish:

| Element | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Causal dependency | What previous event makes this one possible or necessary? |
| Motivational dependency | Why does this person choose this response? |
| Practical dependency | What knowledge, skill, permission, resource, time, or help enables it? |
| Audience dependency | What must the audience already understand to experience its significance? |

A causal outline can still fail if motivational links are arbitrary or enabling conditions appear from nowhere. Conversely, a psychologically convincing decision may be ineffective because the character lacks a required opportunity. Repair the actual gap instead of adding an emotional monologue to a logistical problem.

Use “therefore” and “but” as occasional diagnostic prompts, never as a requirement that every chapter directly cause the next. A subplot can proceed independently; a storm can arrive without the protagonist creating it. The stronger test is whether the sequence accumulates consequences and whether its major coincidences fit the work's established form.

Keep the story world's chronology separate from presentation order. Suspense, mystery, flashback, and dramatic irony redistribute knowledge without changing when past events occurred. If the reader learns a murderer's identity after the arrest, the revelation did not cause the arrest. A correct outline can identify both causal chronology and the chosen order of disclosure.

## Complicate progressively

Progressive complications make the pursuit harder, riskier, and less solvable by the previous approach. They accumulate consequences and mounting antagonism. The standard is changed difficulty, not an unbroken increase in volume, violence, or the number of obstacles.

McKee's progression and Coyne's reversibility question share a useful demand: why can the protagonist no longer solve this with the earlier, cheaper action? Make the answer visible.

Different ways to progress include:

- **Close a route.** The last public denial destroys the possibility of settling the matter privately.
- **Change the kind of problem.** Finding the missing work reveals that another person's authorship must be acknowledged.
- **Make success bind.** The free clinic succeeds, and patients begin relying on its continued existence.
- **Create exposure.** A tactic works but teaches the opposition how the protagonist operates.
- **Activate another level of conflict.** Professional obedience now conflicts with a promise to a friend.
- **Make a resource costly.** The only credible witness requires protection the protagonist cannot provide alone.
- **Reverse an alliance.** An ally supports the goal but opposes the means.
- **Capture the remedy.** Reporting a theft alerts the institution that profits from it.
- **Turn method against desire.** Measures taken to preserve a family make honest intimacy impossible.
- **Bring a delayed cost due.** An earlier rescue leaves someone alive who can now demand the promised repayment.

Choose complications from the story's actual people and conditions. A random attacker can raise danger without developing the governing conflict. A helpful reply can transform it: the protagonist wins permission but must now let someone else lead the project.

Success should sometimes remain success. If every pleasure instantly conceals punishment, the reader learns to distrust all positive movement and the rhythm flattens. Let a victory provide competence, rest, intimacy, or wonder; let its changed conditions matter later. Progression can create commitment without exposing the good itself as fraudulent.

Avoid repeated refusal disguised by scenery. Three officials saying no, three near-identical chases, or three scenes of a lover almost confessing need a developing relation. Perhaps each refusal removes a different evasion; perhaps the third chase reverses who pursues whom. If order can be shuffled without meaningful damage, inspect whether the middle is accumulating or merely occupying time.

Irreversibility is concrete, not a melodramatic adjective. What cannot be recalled, unlearned, unspent, unpromised, or restored without a changed relationship? Even where an outward condition can be repaired, its history may remain consequential. A returned key does not erase what happened while it was withheld.

## Organize scales of consequence

Let beats compose scenes, scenes compose larger movements, and those movements carry the story to culmination. The units are distinguished by consequence and emphasis, not just location or page count.

A scene changes the terms of an encounter. A sequence settles a subordinate undertaking: obtaining access, winning an ally, surviving a crossing, testing a plan. An act changes the governing situation enough to require a substantially different phase of pursuit. The whole story delivers the decisive change in the central value.

The units need not be identical little machines. Every sentence does not require its own crisis; every chapter need not climax at equal intensity. A strong large turn depends on lesser movements that make its importance legible. If every scene claims to be the greatest possible catastrophe, the story loses hierarchy.

Use a structural template when it helps the requested form, especially to expose a missing beginning, development, or payoff. Treat ratios and beat positions as adjustable planning aids. Do not distort a working tragedy, short story, mystery, or episodic design to force identical act arithmetic.

For a midpoint or other major turn, name the functional change: a false victory, discovered antagonist, public commitment, reversal of pursuit, new scale of harm, or collapse of the original plan. “The midpoint happens here” is not a justification. The event earns its place by changing the story's operative question.

Build contrast in tempo and texture. After a major reversal the reader may need time to discover what the new condition feels like. Processing a loss can create the next decision; a quiet sequence can deepen what later action puts at stake. Preserve such movement when it is doing observable experiential work. “Breather” does not excuse a passage with no life of its own.

## Weave subplots and distributed agency

Give a subplot a desire, consequential change, and relation to the larger work. Useful relations include causal complication, thematic contradiction, variation, exposure of costs, preparation of an enabling condition, and a changed perspective on the same event.

Do not make every side character solve a convenient piece of the protagonist's problem. People may contribute for conflicting reasons, refuse an expected role, or pay unequal costs. A final action can concentrate distributed work without making the central person its sole author.

For each crossing between lines, identify what transfers: information, money, obligation, authority, a person, an object, a misunderstanding, a danger, or an expectation. “The romance influences the investigation” is too vague. “He cannot use her testimony without exposing the affair to the person financing his case” is a causal knot.

Subplots may contradict the main plot. If delegation saves the main project while distributed responsibility ruins another, let the difference sharpen the question of accountable cooperation. Do not quietly redefine the failed group as selfish so that the favored principle never encounters a difficult case.

Time crossings for pressure and comprehension. Cutting away immediately before an answer can create suspense; doing it repeatedly can feel like withholding payment. A slower line can prepare attachment before its relevance is visible, but it still owes the reader a present experience. Let the main pursuit and secondary pursuits differ in tempo when their interaction benefits.

Resolve side movements in the order their consequences require. Some combine with the central climax; some conclude earlier; some must register afterward. Forced synchronization can make a living cast resemble a mechanism in which every life finishes on the same cue.

## Design the crisis as a decision

The crisis concentrates the strongest antagonism into a consequential commitment. Its alternatives should differ in the values, costs, and futures they preserve. The decision can be instantaneous in presentation; the conflict must already be intelligible.

A useful dilemma is between incompatible goods, between harms, or between mixed options. “Save the child or be selfish” is usually an easy moral label. “Expose the child's hiding place to secure medicine, or preserve the refuge while the child worsens” identifies actual competing conditions. The form may be a moral dilemma, strategic commitment, refusal, acceptance of irreversible uncertainty, or tragic impasse.

Audit the choice from the protagonist's position:

- Do they know the alternatives exist?
- Can they attempt them with the capacities and resources available?
- Do they understand enough of the likely consequences for the choice to mean what the narration claims?
- What valuable thing makes each alternative difficult to surrender?
- What apparently easy third option has the story established, eliminated, or simply forgotten?
- Why can the commitment no longer be delayed without consequence?

The audience may know more than the protagonist, producing dread or tragic irony. Keep their knowledge separate. Do not characterize an unknowingly harmful act as a deliberate sacrifice, or an unavailable escape as a failure of courage.

The crisis need not consist of a solitary hero declaring a lesson. It can be a public promise, a vote with dissent, a refusal to issue an order, a shared decision, or one person's choice to place their future in another's hands. Show who decides what and who bears the result.

A steadfast character can already know the right commitment. Their crisis may lie in bearing its cost or discovering an effective way to honor it. Do not insert last-minute moral uncertainty merely to supply a required “lie versus truth” beat.

## Deliver the climax as action and result

The crisis is the decision. The climax is the action and result that deliver the governing value's maximum, irreversible charge within this story. “She decides to tell the truth” stops early if the work has made the consequences of telling the central question.

The action must use established capacities and conditions. A confession matters because someone hears it, because its credibility has a basis, and because it changes what another person can do. Moral eloquence cannot substitute for those links. If refusal is the action, establish what would otherwise have happened and how refusing changes the outcome.

The decisive result can fulfill, defeat, transform, or outlast the protagonist's original desire. An investigator can fail to secure a conviction while revealing why justice is impossible within the available institutions. A rescuer can die after making another person's survival possible. A protagonist can relinquish an object that would destroy the value it promised.

One final expectation–result gap can concentrate the entire design. The act succeeds in an unforeseen way, produces a meaningful double consequence, or proves the protagonist's control insufficient. The reversal should draw on accumulated causes, not an unexplained exception introduced at the last moment.

Distributed help and grace are available. Ask what earlier choices preserved, enabled, invited, or failed to command. A rescue can be dramatically earned because another person independently chooses to honor a relationship. It need not be earned through the protagonist's mastery of that person's will. See [lotr-case-study.md](lotr-case-study.md) for mercy remaining consequential beyond Frodo's final capacity.

The end of the line is specific to the governing undertaking. It does not mean no future problem can occur. Identify what is now settled, impossible, irrevocably disclosed, surrendered, or transformed. If the reader is still awaiting the decisive action the story most strongly promised, the ending has probably stopped before its climax.

## Give consequences the right ending space

After the decisive result, choose what must be received: the practical aftermath, the cost, the new relationship, the spread of consequences, the possibility of continuation. A single gesture may suffice; a long novel may need substantial aftermath.

Distinguish completing an action from proving the durability of a condition. Opening a school once establishes an opening; a later ordinary morning can establish a working practice. One apology establishes an attempt at repair, not forgiveness. An ending should not silently claim more than its selected duration shows.

For irony, preserve the gain and loss distinctly. A public triumph does not repay a private betrayal automatically. Let the party and the empty room both exist. For tragedy, do not require a redemptive consolation the work has not earned. For a positive ending, do not add an arbitrary death to make happiness seem respectable.

An open ending should specify the kind of openness. The external result may be known while its valuation remains unsettled; the choice may be made while its long-term consequences remain unknown; several futures may remain possible after a completed encounter. An omitted answer is not automatically a profound one.

## Worked outline: a success that creates the next problem

This original example starts with a musician, not a catastrophe.

**Premise:** Ada, a theater pianist whose work is usually anonymous, wants her own piece included in the final performance before the building closes. She believes recognition must be secured before anyone can take it away. Luis, the stage carpenter, helped her develop its distinctive rhythm while they worked late.

1. **An opportunity changes the balance.** The director offers Ada the closing number after hearing a rehearsal. Her private pleasure becomes a public undertaking.
2. **Her first tactic works.** Ada submits the score under her name. The director loves it. Recognition is no longer hypothetical; programs go to print.
3. **Success creates reliance.** Luis builds a moving platform timed to their rhythm, assuming their collaboration will be acknowledged. Ada praises the platform while avoiding the authorship question.
4. **An attempted repair deepens the commitment.** She adds a dedication to Luis. He explains that a dedication makes him the recipient of a favor rather than a maker of the work. The apparent generous solution exposes the real dispute.
5. **Opposition acquires practical force.** The director warns that changing the billing now will jeopardize Ada's promised introduction to a producer who wants a singular discovery. The threat is real; the producer has other choices.
6. **An independent life resists.** Luis declines to operate the platform. He offers the safety notes so the performance need not fail. His refusal protects a boundary without becoming a villainous attempt to ruin Ada.
7. **The cheaper options close.** Another operator can manage the platform, but Ada discovers that the musical transition works only because Luis has been adjusting it in response to her playing. They have been collaborating more deeply than the submitted score admits.
8. **Crisis.** Ada can perform a simplified solo version under her existing credit, preserving a credible career opportunity, or publicly name the collaboration and ask Luis whether he still wants to perform. The second choice offers no guarantee of his agreement or the producer's approval.
9. **Climactic action and result.** She corrects the introduction before playing. Luis chooses to join from the wings; the performance succeeds through their responsive work. The producer leaves before the curtain call. Shared authorship becomes visible; Ada has relinquished sole credit and accepted career uncertainty. The departure does not, by itself, prove that the introduction is canceled or why the producer left.
10. **Resolution.** They discuss how the piece may be performed elsewhere. Luis changes a transition she dislikes. The final exchange establishes a relationship in which acknowledgment includes the right to disagree.

The complication is not that each rehearsal goes worse. Early success binds Ada to a false public account. Attempts to protect both sole credit and friendship expose their incompatibility under her current terms. Luis retains independent agency; her public correction does not purchase his forgiveness. The clear gain is acknowledged collaboration; the surrendered good is exclusive recognition, while the career consequence remains uncertain. If a definite career loss is intended, enact the withdrawal and establish its cause rather than treating an ambiguous exit as proof. The final ordinary disagreement shows what the new relationship permits.

An alternate tragic version could have Ada keep the sole credit and deliver a brilliant simplified performance. Do not make the music suddenly bad merely to punish her. Her artistic success can coexist with the loss of the relationship that helped make it possible.

## Diagnose the structure before replacing it

Read what exists and identify the scale of failure. A flat scene may need a sharper tactic; a flat middle may need changed consequences; a weak ending may reveal that the object of desire or chief cause was never clear. Do not rebuild the entire premise because one passage is sluggish.

Useful tests are small and discriminating:

- Summarize each major movement by what becomes possible or impossible afterward.
- Trace the crisis alternatives backward to where their values and costs were established.
- Remove the final speech mentally and state what the action itself expresses.
- Inspect repeated scenes for a different dependency, decision, or reader experience.
- Separate the protagonist's contributions from help, institutions, resources, and chance.
- Check whether each apparently decisive turn is actually an event, a discovery, or an interpretation.
- Identify the strongest promised encounter and verify that the ending answers it in some deliberate form.

For a requested outline, return a usable sequence with consequential transitions, not only named beats. For a diagnosis, quote or locate the failing connection and suggest the smallest change that could repair it. For exploration, offer structurally distinct routes with their costs. Let the user's desired form determine how much scaffolding appears in the final deliverable.
