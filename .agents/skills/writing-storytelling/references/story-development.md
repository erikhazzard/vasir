# Developing a story without flattening it

Read for a new story, substantial premise development, or deciding the next useful artifact. For a requested passage, write the passage through its specialist route; do not turn this development method into a prerequisite questionnaire.

## Start with the living material

Stories can begin with a voice, image, situation, person, relationship, injustice, setting, formal experiment, ending, or question. Preserve what makes the starting material alive before translating it into a plot proposition. An image of a daughter washing her dead father's shoes contains touch, labor, intimacy, and an unanswered reason. “A woman processes grief” loses the material before development begins.

Identify the live uncertainty: what the user wants help discovering or making. A request for three endings is not an invitation to replace the protagonist. A request to make an existing cast vivid does not require restarting from genre selection. If the user supplies canon, distinguish it from exploratory suggestions. Make a modest assumption when reversible; ask when a missing answer would create a different work.

Use [core model](core-model.md) if the project needs a shared causal vocabulary. Use [genre and form](genre-and-form.md) when the intended reader experience or medium is unclear. Do not load every domain in case it might matter.

## Establish the contract

Extract the few commitments that will govern choices:

- Requested deliverable and scale: paragraph, scene, character, outline, chapter, complete work, critique.
- Medium, viewpoint, intended experience, and any material audience constraints.
- Facts and effects that must remain: the sister leaves; the ending is hopeful but not reconciliatory; magic cannot revive the dead.
- The part that is still open: what forces departure, whose viewpoint carries it, how hope becomes credible.

An experience is more specific than an adjective. “Dark” might mean dread of contamination, moral complicity, helplessness before an institution, or grief without consolation. Those require different decisions. Offer a concrete reading of the intended experience rather than an exhaustive tone survey.

For a factual narrative, use [applied narrative](applied-narrative.md). Its evidence contract changes what may be invented. For participatory work, use [interactive and adaptation](interactive-and-adaptation.md): player choice is not just an author-controlled protagonist with more branches.

## Find the generative contradiction

A productive premise contains a relation that can keep generating consequential decisions. The person needs something that their current way of getting it endangers; a community's remedy reproduces its problem; a successful caretaker must become less indispensable; a desired revelation threatens the bond that made the search possible.

Do not force a fatal flaw into every person. The contradiction may belong to circumstances, incompatible goods, inadequate institutions, a mistaken public story, or a genuinely unsolvable condition. A sound person in a corrupt system need not secretly deserve the trouble.

Ask what becomes difficult specifically because of who this person is, what they value, and how this world answers. “A courier must cross a war zone” supplies a task. “A courier trusted because she never reads the messages learns that the delivery will name the families hiding her” connects reputation, desire, moral knowledge, and action. Development can now produce choices rather than interchangeable attacks.

If the opening material resists a pursuit, do not discard it. A portrait of neighbors sharing a wall might organize itself around recurrent sounds and changing interpretations. Its development question is how repetition becomes discovery without manufacturing a thriller. Strong form-specific alternatives are addressed in [genre and form](genre-and-form.md).

## Develop alternatives that differ in meaning

When exploring, vary a consequential dimension rather than giving the same idea three costumes.

For the courier premise:

- She disobeys, saves the families, and destroys the neutral network others depended on: protection here creates vulnerability elsewhere.
- She convinces rival couriers to expose the order together: distributed refusal succeeds where solitary purity would fail.
- She obeys while secretly warning the families, but her divided strategy makes neither side trust the warning: evading commitment compounds danger.

These are not improved synonyms. They imply different antagonism, preparation, character decisions, and endings. State each variant's promise and price briefly. Do not fix a number of options or demand paired positive/negative portfolios unless that helps the request.

An apparently weaker candidate may contain the work's distinctive difficulty. Do not choose solely for pitch neatness. Consider which version generates particular relationships, memorable scenes, and a real question the writer wants to remain with.

## Connect character to the pursuit

Give the protagonist an object of desire and an operational philosophy. Establish why the pursuit matters now, what they can actually do, whom they depend on, and which relationships make the stakes more than abstract. Attachment comes from encountered specificity, not a mandatory tragic dossier.

Map only the decisions the next work needs. A useful seed might be:

> She needs the village to accept the warning. She believes neutrality is the only form of trust that survives a war. Her delivery has made neutrality impossible. The baker who hid her will listen only if she identifies her source; the apprentice courier will lose his route if she does.

This creates a cast with incompatible legitimate interests. “Loyal but stubborn, with a secret fear of abandonment” may be usable later, but does not yet generate an event. Use [characters](characters.md) for personhood and attachment; [relationships and cast](relationships-and-cast.md) for a social field; [character arcs](character-arcs.md) for development that need not mirror external success.

## Build the middle through consequences

Develop attempts, not obstacles in isolation. For each substantial movement, connect:

> Current condition → attempted solution → actual response → changed value or capacity → next necessary or newly possible action.

Ask why the next movement cannot simply occur first. If the answer is only that the outline places it later, strengthen causality or choose a deliberately episodic form. Successful attempts can generate the best complications: dependence, exposure, debt, public commitments, or a remedy that cannot scale.

Vary the kind of pressure. Evidence can disprove an assumption. An ally can need incompatible help. Victory can transfer responsibility. The antagonist can adopt the protagonist's method. A promised sacrifice can become another person's unwilling sacrifice. Bigger violence is only one option, and often the less specific one.

Do not turn every complication into punishment. Discovery, generosity, skill, luck, and cooperation can open real possibilities. A story that rejects every reasonable alternative just to corner its protagonist feels rigged. Use [plot and structure](plot-and-structure.md) for sequence construction and [antagonism and irony](antagonism-and-irony.md) for opposition that genuinely tests the emerging argument.

## Treat the ending as a testable hypothesis

Knowing a provisional ending can guide preparation without requiring obedience to it. Name the final action and result, the decisive value change, whose account is settled, and the cost that remains. Then ask what must be true earlier for that event to be possible, intelligible, and emotionally consequential.

Work backward for necessary preparation, then forward to see whether those people would actually take those actions. Backward design alone can create puppet behavior; forward improvisation alone can create events the ending cannot honestly resolve. Let the two checks correct each other.

If the ending relies on help, prepare the helper's capacity, independent motive, and causal route. The protagonist need not perform every decisive action. If the story is about the limits of self-sufficiency, changing the ending into solitary triumph destroys its meaning. Prepared possibility also need not imply guaranteed rescue.

Use [controlling idea](controlling-idea.md) to formulate what the achieved ending says: value, final charge, chief cause. If the desired proposition differs from the enacted cause, revise the proposition or the events. Do not add an explanatory closing speech to conceal the mismatch.

## Move between design and drafting

A premise can look finished while its people have no language. Draft a pressure-bearing encounter early enough to discover voice, behavior, and the kind of time this work wants. A scene can reveal that a supposed antagonist is more compelling than the planned hero, or that the emotional center is an apparently peripheral relationship.

Conversely, vivid pages can postpone the central encounter indefinitely. After drafting, ask what became true, what the reader now anticipates, and whether the next planned event still follows. Design is useful when it changes the next page; pages are useful evidence when they can change the design.

Use exploratory scenes without pretending they are canon. Label a discarded alternative in the project's notes if confusion is likely. Do not keep polishing a beautiful passage whose causal role has vanished merely because it was expensive to write; preserve it separately when valuable.

For dialogue-first work, use [dialogue](dialogue.md) and write the exchange. For paragraph-level craft, use [narration and prose](narration-and-prose.md). A structural audit cannot substitute for either.

## Keep only the memory the work needs

For an extended project, retain a compact, living record in its existing structure. Distinguish established facts, planned facts, disputed interpretations, and discarded alternatives. Useful fields are conditional, not mandatory:

| Record | Keep it when |
| --- | --- |
| Canon and limits | Multiple scenes or collaborators can contradict established facts |
| Value trajectories by person or group | Apparent victory risks hiding another account's loss |
| Knowledge and revelation order | Suspense, mystery, viewpoint, or branches depend on who knows what |
| Promises and preparation | A payoff depends on earlier capacity, motive, object, or audience expectation |
| Relationships and obligations | Favors, betrayals, permissions, debts, or authority change across scenes |
| Open choices | A provisional idea could otherwise harden unnoticed into canon |
| Revision decisions | Later edits must preserve the reason for an earlier solution |

Record the minimum usable fact: “Mara knows the seal is forged; Len does not” is better than a second synopsis. Do not invent numerical emotional states or exhaustive character databases unless the project actually benefits. For interactive state, use the explicit authority distinctions in its reference.

## Deliver the right artifact

For a premise request, return a premise with its generative tension, not a whole production bible. For a new outline, show consequential progression and enough scene specificity to make the mechanism inspectable. For a draft, give the reader the writing, not a running commentary explaining that each line enacts a tactic. For diagnosis, prioritize the failure with the greatest downstream effect and demonstrate the smallest convincing repair.

Finish with the real unresolved choice only if one remains material. Do not ask the user to approve every ordinary creative decision, and do not declare a story excellent because its worksheet is complete. The test is the experience and possibilities the artifact now creates.
