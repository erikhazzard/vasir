# Horror

Use when the central encounter threatens life through a frightening violation of bodily, mental, social, or spiritual security. **Value spectrum:** life/death, extending to a **fate worse than death** such as enduring captivity, damnation, or destruction of identity. **Promised emotion:** fear, including dread. A dangerous creature alone can belong to action; ask what makes survival itself uncertain, corrupted, or intolerable here.

The protagonist seeks survival, understanding, protection of others, or neutralization of the threat. The **monster/villain** embodies the horror; **victims** make consequences particular. A capable protagonist can still lack necessary power or knowledge. Do not manufacture vulnerability by making everyone ignore available escape routes.

## Core event and diagnostic expectations

**The Confrontation with Evil:** the protagonist faces the horror in the decisive encounter. “Confrontation” can mean resisting, escaping, bargaining, or discovering the full cost of survival; physical victory is only one possible outcome.

Choose the information relationship: shared discovery admits reader and character into danger together; foreknowledge lets readers anticipate a character's mistake; uncertain interpretation makes evidence oscillate. Concealing the monster is not automatically stronger.

- Establish the ordinary good invaded, then what isolation removes: witnesses, aid, credibility, or an exit. Escalation should change exposure or compromise a usable protection, not merely add frightening images.
- Let discovery change the survival problem. Encountering the horrific, recognizing the monster's behavior, and grasping its larger implications can coincide. Distinguish observed survival rules from conjecture without explaining ultimate origins. Prepare any rule the escape depends on; rule instability can itself supply horror when uncertainty belongs to the promise.
- Test increasing menace, the protagonist at the monster's mercy, and shattered security. If competent adaptations fail, show the changed condition or the threat's response that defeats them.
- A false ending can expose an unresolved danger after apparent safety. Use it when supported by the threat's established possibilities; an arbitrary resurrection spends the credibility of earlier consequences.
- Resolve the ordeal through escape, defeat, death, or changed existence. Track what survives and what is irretrievably violated; biological survival may carry the negative charge. Give aftermath enough duration that successive shocks do not erase their damage.

For concrete information arrangements, practical threat rules, and an adapting pursuer, read [suspense, mystery, and horror](../suspense-mystery-and-horror.md).

## Choose the avenue of fear

- **Supernatural:** unseen agencies breach the world's ordinary protections; decide what evidence makes their influence actionable.
- **Psychological:** perception, trust, or mental autonomy become insecure; preserve the difference between uncertain interpretation and arbitrary events.
- **Body:** transformation or destruction threatens ownership of one's physical self.
- **Monster:** the creature's presence, behavior, and reach organize the threat.
- **Gothic:** inherited secrets and the past entangle romance, melodrama, and present danger, often in a historical setting.
- **Slasher:** stalking and successive attacks change a group's remaining protection and escape possibilities.

**Provisional controlling idea:** Life becomes unending captivity because the caretaker surrenders the only exit in exchange for continued visits from the dead child. The negative final charge follows an enacted bargain; the story must establish why surrendering that exit binds the caretaker.
