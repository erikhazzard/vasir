# Western

Use when a protector's individual sovereignty is tested by a community's need for defense against subjugation. Frontier scenery alone does not establish this conflict.

## Promise and engine

- **Value spectrum:** freedom ↔ subjugation. Track the community's freedom separately from the protector's autonomy; saving one may cost the other.
- **Promised emotion:** intrigue about how an independent person will contend with coercive power and social obligation.
- **Core event:** **the Big Showdown**, which makes the struggle between protector and oppressor decisive. Its function matters more than a literal gunfight.
- **Desire and roles:** the hero seeks to save threatened people from the villain's control. The hero, victim, and villain form the guide's central triangle. Give the protected people consequential choices rather than treating them only as evidence of the hero's goodness.

## Conventions and obligatory functions

Make the frontier's freedom and isolation consequential: distance, exposure, or unreliable law should affect available actions. A substantial power divide makes intervention costly. When the hero operates outside the law, establish what lawful protection cannot accomplish and what that independence risks. Give the antagonist a concrete objective—the guide's “MacGuffin”—and establish their formidable reach through credible reputation or demonstrated power.

Find these functions in the story's own arrangement:

- An attack or encroachment makes oppression unavoidable; reluctance exposes the cost of accepting responsibility.
- Engagement and discovery clarify what the antagonist wants. Failed tactics change the protector's options, rather than merely supplying another fight.
- Severe defeat tests whether the community can be defended without reproducing domination or surrendering everything the hero values.
- The showdown resolves the confrontation; aftermath establishes what the sacrifice actually purchased. Departure, belonging, loss, or ambiguous fulfillment must follow from that result.

Do not turn sacrificial protection into compulsory assimilation. A steadfast outsider can defend a community without discovering that independence was a moral flaw.

## Subgenre choices

Design implications drawn from the guide's sketches:

- **Vengeance:** make retribution's effect on freedom decisive; defeating an oppressor can leave the avenger captive to revenge.
- **Transition:** let changing institutions, alliances, and stories of the past determine who gains freedom.
- **Professional:** test chosen obligations against payment, private advantage, and communal need.

**Provisional controlling idea — illustrative synthesis:** The town's freedom is preserved when a hired defender forfeits payment to break the rancher's armed monopoly. **Value:** freedom. **Final charge:** preserved. **Chief cause:** costly intervention that removes the means of coercion.
