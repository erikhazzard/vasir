# Society

Use when the central struggle concerns who exercises power within a social order, and whose exclusion sustains it. Private disputes become Society conflicts when outcomes change institutional control or a group's position.

## Promise and engine

- **Value spectrum:** power ↔ impotence. Specify power over what: decisions, resources, rights, public recognition, or the ability to organize.
- **Promised emotion:** intrigue through exposed hypocrisy, contested authority, secrets, and shifting alliances.
- **Core event:** **Revolution**—a consequential transfer, seizure, or loss of power. Show who can now do something previously denied, and who can no longer command it.
- **Desire and roles:** a disenfranchised protagonist or group seeks recognition, rights, justice, or replacement of the prevailing order. The tyrant maintains control; rebels organize opposition. Recognition becomes causally significant when acknowledgment changes actual standing or capacity to act.

## Conventions and obligatory functions

The guide favors a large power divide and a broad social canvas. Use an ensemble to reveal different interests within the conflict; supporting people need desires beyond mirroring the lead's personality. Make the institution's means of maintaining power visible, so removing one officeholder does not automatically resolve an intact system.

Find these functions in the work's chosen arrangement:

- A threat or opportunity disturbs reigning power. Reluctance and forced response reveal what action costs from the protagonist's position in the hierarchy.
- Opposition learns the ruler's concrete objective. An initial strategy fails, exposing the limits of its existing influence.
- A point of no return makes ordinary accommodation unavailable; severe reversal forces a consequential change of method or commitment.
- Revolution tests the opposition's actual capacity. Aftermath shows the new distribution of power and its personal, relational, and institutional costs.

The guide emphasizes exile and bittersweet outcomes. Treat these as prompts to dramatize the defeated party's loss and the victor's price; neither compulsory banishment nor vague “complexity” substitutes for consequences. Personal reconciliation cannot by itself establish institutional change.

## Political focus

**Political** is the guide's developed subgenre here: gaining, retaining, or losing power. Decide whether the ending redistributes effective authority or merely replaces its holder. The guide's claims about autocracy, pluralism, and conformity are candidate fictional arguments, not established social laws.

**Provisional controlling idea — illustrative synthesis:** Workers gain power when coordinated refusal forces the council to grant enforceable representation. **Value:** power. **Final charge:** gained. **Chief cause:** collective leverage converted into durable decision rights.
