# Interactive narrative and adaptation

Read for branching fiction, game scenes and quests, tabletop situations, or translating a story into another medium. This reference owns narrative decisions and their consequences. It is not a replacement for implementation expertise in persistence, multiplayer state, accessibility, or production systems.

## Four intentions, not one protagonist

In authored prose, the writer arranges a character's choices and their consequences. In interactive work, distinguish:

- The character's fictional desire and knowledge.
- The player's intention, which may include exploration, experimentation, roleplay, optimization, or refusing the premise.
- The designer's intended experience or thematic question.
- The system's actual incentives, permissions, and recorded results.

A character can lament domination while the game makes domination the only reliable way to progress. The enacted incentives then contest the spoken theme. Decide whether that tension is deliberate criticism, an unresolved design conflict, or merely two teams using different assumptions. Do not solve it by giving the character another moral speech.

Reader participation in interpreting a novel is real, but it differs from operational control. Calling both “agency” without qualification hides whether an action can change an outcome. Conversely, operational agency is not unlimited authorship: a player can have rich tactical or relational choices inside a disclosed fixed historical event.

## Establish what participants can change

Before writing branches, identify the domain of choice: expression, method, relationship, resources, local outcome, shared world, or final ending. Specify what is fixed and what the work promises will respond. The scope can be modest if it is legible and honored.

A tragedy known to end in the city burning can still offer consequential choices about whom to accompany, what to preserve, what truth to tell, and who will bear the cost. A game advertised as allowing the player to prevent the fire breaks a different promise if every successful intervention is secretly nullified.

Use the smallest state model that protects those promises. For each material choice, identify the available information, recognized action, immediate response, persistent effect, later acknowledgment, and what remains unchanged. For a short interactive scene, a few explicit facts suffice. For a live shared world, implementation must define authority and persistence beyond this writing reference.

Do not require every choice to create a unique ending. Do require the kind of effect that the choice led the player to expect. A different line can be sufficient for an expressive choice; it is insufficient for a promised rescue outcome.

## Build situations that can answer action

An interactive situation needs people with motives, actionable circumstances, constraints, and plausible responses. It is more robust than a sequence that depends on the player feeling exactly what the author expects.

Invented example: a ferry captain will evacuate one district before the tide closes the channel. The inland shelter has room but no medical supplies; the harbor has supplies but an unreliable loading crane. The player can repair, negotiate, redirect labor, expose a concealed boat, or choose who travels first if those actions belong to the available mechanics.

The situation should distinguish failure to imagine an action from a world where the action is impossible. If the crane cannot be repaired in time, make the relevant constraint available. If a clever combination can solve both problems within established rules, let it work or revise the premise before play. Do not increase the tide secretly because the authored dilemma demands suffering.

Progressive complications still matter, but the system must derive them from what actually happened. Saving the harbor first may leave the shelter without a trusted coordinator; saving the shelter first may lose supplies. A cooperative success can generate a different next question instead of being retroactively sabotaged.

## Branch without forgetting the choice

Reconvergence saves production work; it need not erase history. Two routes can reach the same council meeting while differing in allies, losses, knowledge, credibility, or debt. Write the shared event so those differences remain consequential where relevant.

| Earlier choice | Shared next scene | Difference the scene must retain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Used the captain's hidden reserve boat | Council decides next evacuation | More survivors; captain's deception exposed; trust contested |
| Preserved the secret to keep the captain cooperating | Same council decision | Fewer seats available; captain remains influential; player carries knowledge |
| Organized volunteer crews without the captain | Same council decision | Independent capacity exists; volunteers expect a say |

This is not a demand for all three routes in every game. It demonstrates a distinction between shared location and identical state. If the narrative cannot afford the implied consequences, change the offered choice or its framing before promising them.

Branches should vary the decision or experience, not merely swap affectionate and rude wording before an unchanged response. For [character arcs](character-arcs.md), decide whether development is authored, selected, inferred from conduct, or left to the player's interpretation. Do not announce that the player learned a lesson they deliberately rejected.

## Dialogue as an available act

A menu label is an action promise. “Ask what happened” should not unexpectedly perform an accusation unless the interface clearly marks uncertain social interpretation as part of play. Distinguish a character misunderstanding the utterance from the system substituting an unchosen utterance.

Apply [dialogue](dialogue.md) to each speaker's immediate intention, background desires, and capacity to respond. An NPC's refusal can be dramatically good and operationally honest. Do not make every personality a lock opened by the same high-empathy sentence or highest persuasion statistic.

Track knowledge when dialogue depends on it. A line can be unavailable because the character lacks information; it should not be unavailable merely because the writer wants a reveal later while the player has already established the fact. If player and character knowledge intentionally diverge, make the contract clear.

Allow silence, leaving, assistance, waiting, or direct candor when supported by the medium. Not every meaningful conversation needs an adversarial choice wheel. For expressive scenes, an acknowledged way of responding can matter without changing the plot's destination.

## Secrets, retries, and remembered consequences

Keep concealed facts consistent with established effects. A tabletop secret's delivery location may remain flexible before it has constrained the world. Once a poison has distinctive symptoms and access requires a known key, relocating responsibility cannot contradict those facts merely to surprise the player.

Separate four records when relevant: world truth, character knowledge, player knowledge, and displayed recap. An unreliable character can lie; an authoritative quest log should not silently rewrite what happened unless its unreliability is itself part of the established form.

Retries introduce another distinction. A player may remember a failed route while the fictional protagonist does not. A time-loop story may make both remember. A checkpoint may restore resources but retain an unlocked informational clue. State the intended semantics so prose does not accidentally treat replay knowledge as a supernatural ability.

Surprise should survive scrutiny of the rules that generated it. A hidden contingency can be compelling; a rule invented only after the player succeeds makes apparent causality untrustworthy.

## Tabletop preparation without predetermined behavior

Prepare the opening disturbance, people and motives, actionable locations, clues or secrets, and pressures that will advance if unopposed. Prepare possible scenes as expendable material, not an itinerary the players must enact.

The facilitator controls what is theirs to control: a guard's response, a storm's established danger, an institution's policy, a villain's next attempt. The players control their characters' choices within the agreed game. Inviting a player to describe a local custom does not automatically grant control of every canonical fact; agreeing to a dangerous premise does not grant the facilitator control over how the character feels about it.

For a crisis to matter, refusal and alternative action must have intelligible consequences. If the adventure requires the group to accept a quest, establish that premise out of character rather than manufacturing increasingly coercive coincidences until they comply. If they solve the immediate problem early, spend the available time on consequences, another live uncertainty, or a satisfying shorter session.

Improvisation can preserve prepared meaning without preserving prepared events. A concern about entrusted power can appear in whichever authority relationship the players actually develop. Do not force every independent subplot to vindicate a fixed controlling idea.

## Persistent and procedural stories

A persistent world needs finite closures inside ongoing change. Resolve the promise a player has actually invested in; do not keep every relationship and mystery permanently incomplete to preserve future content. A sequel can introduce a new problem without declaring the previous victory meaningless.

A generator can create occasions for stories without preauthoring each story's meaning. Design legible agents, differentiated motives, consequential resources, remembered relations, and constraints that produce intelligible interactions. Then test actual generated sequences: do they develop, or repeat the same betrayal with different names?

For generated dialogue, expressive freedom must not silently create world facts, rewards, permissions, or completed quests. Keep authoritative state distinct from proposed speech. The writing question is what the character can truthfully know, promise, infer, or conceal; the engineering question is how the system enforces that boundary.

Persistent multiplayer architecture has additional requirements—shared versus personal state, returning players, unavailable content, localization, moderation, and rollback. Hand those requirements to the appropriate implementation or architecture expertise. Do not make a short quest request pay for a live-service operating model.

## Adapt the operation, not just the content

First identify what the source operation accomplishes. A paragraph of interior narration may disclose a lie the speaker tells themself, create intimacy, slow time, or provide factual context. The adaptation needs that effect, not necessarily a voice-over containing the same words.

For each material change, track source event or device, audience function, target-medium equivalent, lost affordance, and resulting change in meaning. Use this only at the scale needed. In a film, a withheld glance can carry a recognition; on stage, shared space and an overheard exchange can make exposure immediate; in a game, requiring the player to perform the routine can disclose its cost.

Do not assume adding choice always improves the source. If a scene's meaning depends on witnessing an unavoidable loss, offering a fake prevention choice may damage it. Conversely, a story about complicity can gain force when participation genuinely matters. Preserve the user's canon and fidelity priorities; label deliberate departures rather than importing film events into a novel analysis unnoticed.

## A compact narrative test

Play or walk through at least two materially different choices and one sensible unplanned attempt. At each point, compare what the participant knew, what they selected, what happened, and what the next scene remembers. Examine a refusal, an early success, and any retry if those are supported.

Then read the actual prose or dialogue. Coherent flags do not make a moving encounter. Mechanics, expression, and remembered consequence must meet in the player's experience; a design document alone does not demonstrate that they do.
