## Value Spectrum — Operational Synthesis

The supplied source names the global poles. The deeper states below operationalize McKee’s contrary, contradictory, and negation-of-negation progression for this genre; tailor the exact wording to the story without leaving the canonical axis.

| Level | Genre state |
|---|---|
| Positive | Life preserved, restored, or secured |
| Contrary | Jeopardy, injury, exposure, or escalating vulnerability |
| Contradictory | Death or irreversible destruction |
| Negation of the negation | The hero preserves self by sacrificing the victim, becomes the life-destroying villain, or creates mass death while claiming to save life |

## Core Roles — Source-Derived

- **Hero:** Embodies altruism and risks or sacrifices life to save others.
- **Villain:** Embodies narcissism and sacrifices others for personal gain, power, or domination.
- **Victim:** Is vulnerable and cannot defeat the villain without the hero; vulnerability is not the same as cowardice.

## Core-Emotion Techniques — Source-Derived

1. Rebellion against authority
2. Conquering limitations
3. Coping with frustration
4. Exploring the unknown
5. Breaking taboos

## Genre Conventions — Source-Derived

- An intense, disturbed physical and social setting that naturally generates danger.
- Dueling hierarchies: growth/altruism versus dominance/power.
- A hero who accepts a challenge created by the villain and pursues the rescue or preservation of life.
- A villain with substantially greater resources and power.
- A victim or threatened community who cannot self-rescue from the central force.
- A speech or demonstration in praise of the villain that establishes apparent invincibility and reveals why the antagonist will not yield.
- A deadline or clock that limits the hero’s time to act.
- Set-piece sequences that expose strengths and weaknesses and develop the skills needed for the final tactic.
- A fast-paced plot that forces escalating risk.

## Obligatory Moments — Source-Derived, Structurally Normalized

1. An inciting attack or threat by the villain.
2. The hero sidesteps responsibility to act.
3. Forced out of the ordinary world, the hero lashes out or responds through an inadequate strategy.
4. The protagonist discovers and understands the antagonist’s MacGuffin or object of desire.
5. The hero’s initial strategy fails.
6. At the All Is Lost moment, the hero realizes the approach must change to preserve life.
7. Hero at the Mercy of the Villain: the core event in which the hero’s gift and final tactic must be expressed.
8. The hero’s sacrifice is rewarded—or, in a negative ending, the refusal/corruption of sacrifice causes Death to prevail.

## Conscious Objects of Desire — Source-Derived / Consolidated

- Defeat the antagonist or evil force
- Save an individual, group, society, or environment from harm
- Secure or destroy a dangerous MacGuffin
- Restore order and peace

## Genre Spine

The hero pursues the conscious object of desire through cycles of escalating antagonism. The villain stays ahead through a rational, probable, dangerous, rewarding, character-specific plan. The hero’s initial tactics fail until a corrected strategy, gift, or sacrifice enables the final table-turning action.

## Twenty-Skeletal-Scene Map — Operational Normalization

The supplied notes often use “climax” for a decision, condition, or encounter. This map enforces:

- **Crisis = choice.**
- **Climax = enacted answer.**
- **Resolution = consequence.**

### Beginning Hook

| Command | Genre-specific function |
|---|---|
| Inciting Incident | The villain attacks or threatens life. |
| Turning Point | The protagonist recognizes the disorder and true scale of the threat. |
| Crisis | Avoid responsibility and preserve the ordinary life, or engage and accept danger. |
| Climax | The protagonist commits to engage through an initial tactic. |
| Resolution | The immediate threat is contained or the hero enters a fix-it-and-forget-it mission that proves inadequate. |
### Middle Build One

| Command | Genre-specific function |
|---|---|
| Inciting Incident | The hero enters the new arena and sees the villain’s reach. |
| Turning Point | The protagonist becomes the villain’s target or loses safety. |
| Crisis | Comply to reduce danger, or defy and accept the point of no return. |
| Climax | The hero defies through the initial strategy. |
| Resolution | The villain asserts superior power and closes escape routes. |
### Middle Build Two

| Command | Genre-specific function |
|---|---|
| Inciting Incident | The hero discovers the plan, MacGuffin, or victim’s true stakes. |
| Turning Point | The initial strategy catastrophically fails; All Is Lost. |
| Crisis | Give up or commit to a sacrifice that may make death meaningful and preserve others. |
| Climax | The hero accepts the corrected strategy and irreversible sacrifice. |
| Resolution | The hero prepares to enter the ultimate arena with the final tactic. |
### Ending Payoff

| Command | Genre-specific function |
|---|---|
| Inciting Incident | The hero enters the villain’s sanctum or the deadline collapses. |
| Turning Point | A death, betrayal, or reversal leaves the hero at the villain’s mercy. |
| Crisis | Choose between the final incompatible values, often whether the desired end justifies a life-destroying means. |
| Climax | The hero executes the prepared final tactic, expresses the gift, pays the sacrifice, and turns—or fails to turn—the tables. |
| Resolution | Life is preserved or Death prevails; the sacrifice is rewarded, corrupted, or rendered futile according to the controlling idea. |

## Adjacent-Genre Disambiguation

- Choose Thriller when a master villain personalizes the threat, the protagonist becomes the victim, investigation and a false ending dominate, and the final value is Life/Damnation.
- Choose Horror when fear, helplessness, discovery of the horrific, and a fate worse than death dominate the audience promise.
- Choose War when organized conflict and the preservation or loss of Honor—not survival itself—control the final meaning.

## Controlling-Idea Value Rules

- The controlling idea must begin from Life or Death: “Life is preserved…,” “Life survives…,” “Death prevails…,” or an equivalent charged form.
- Courage, justice, freedom, truth, unity, and sacrifice may appear only in the Cause or secondary charges.
- A positive ending requires the final action to preserve life. A negative ending requires the causal chain to produce death or irreversible destruction, not merely sadness or moral ambiguity.

### Positive Shell

> **Life [is secured / restored / achieved / preserved] when [specific causal agent] [performs a story-specific climactic action] because [character-specific realization, steadfast value, or sacrifice].**

### Negative Shell

> **Death [prevails / deepens / is imposed] when [specific causal agent] [chooses, refuses, corrupts, or fails through a story-specific action].**

The shell is a diagnostic scaffold, not finished prose. Replace generic verbs with the exact causal mechanism.
