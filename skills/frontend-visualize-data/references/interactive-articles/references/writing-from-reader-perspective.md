Each mandate says where readers look for something and which habit it breaks. Whatever changes, keep every fact, step, number and caveat. Leave no bracket or blank for the reader to fill when the missing detail is yours to supply: write the line so it reads right without it (a letter needs no "Dear [name]", and "the next board meeting" needs no date). A value the reader must supply, such as their own API key in a command, stays a placeholder.

1. Connect the facts in sentences. Readers expect each sentence to say how it attaches to the last; otherwise they must build the reasoning themselves.

   Default: true statements stacked under labels ("Wind.", "Terrain."), never showing how the factors "feed one another".

   Instead: let order and a repeated key term carry most links. Where a reader would miss how one fact causes, limits or contradicts another, say so in the sentence (because, so, but, unless), never with a heading, bullet or "also". Name at most one relation per sentence, don't string because, so and therefore through a paragraph, and invent no link.

   Before: The migration reset everyone's saved filters on Tuesday. Support tickets doubled this week.

   After: Tuesday's migration reset everyone's saved filters, so this week's support tickets doubled.

2. Give each form only its job. Readers take layout as a signal: numbers say order matters, and a vertical list says each item earns its own line.

   Default: one skeleton for every topic (definition, bold-labeled blocks, takeaway), even for an ordered pipeline or a web of causes.

   Instead: number steps the reader will carry out, and any chain of stages they must keep in order, even inside an explanation. Table what they will compare or look up, and list parallel items needing no discussion. Give headings only to parts the reader will jump between; a short piece has few, but a standalone piece keeps its title. Write the reasoning as paragraphs, never recite a table's rows again in prose, and keep reference tables, tutorial steps and code intact.

   Before: To reset a breaker, push its handle fully off, then back on.

   After: 1. Push the breaker handle fully off. 2. Push it back on.

3. Develop each paragraph to its point. Readers expect a paragraph to keep moving after its first claim and to land its point after the setup or at the end.

   Default: a claim, two or three sentences only confirming it ("Wind is the strongest accelerant..."), and perhaps a stray fact.

   Instead: make each later sentence add a step the claim lacked (a consequence, cost, limit or decision) from the material. Give each paragraph one point: when a new point starts, start a new paragraph, and split any paragraph past about 120 words. Move a trailing fact to the paragraph that uses it, or give it a short paragraph of its own; never drop it.

   Before: The new payroll vendor would save us money. It is cheaper than our current one.

   After: The new payroll vendor would save $6,000 a year. Its phone support closes at 5 p.m. Eastern, though, before our Pacific office finishes payroll.

4. End on what the body earned but has not said. Readers weigh a piece's last sentences heavily and grow impatient once they see a recap coming.

   Default: a labeled closing block ("Summary", "The takeaway", "Why it matters") that re-lists the parts or restates the framing.

   Instead: drop the label and close on a decision, rule of thumb or consequence the reader can use, keeping any fact the recap held. A limit closes the piece only with what to do about it, and the last sentence never promises more to come. Recap only on request or after a long, dense body. A reference entry needs no ending.

   Before: In summary, the new lease has costs and benefits, and the right choice depends on our priorities.

   After: Sign the new lease by March 1 if its $40,000 saving matters more than a longer commute for six of us; otherwise renew the old one for a year.

5. Join paragraphs at their edges. Readers carry a paragraph's last words into the next and expect its opening to pick up that strand.

   Default: each block opens on a fresh label and noun, or links back three blocks ("Burning embers..." right after terrain).

   Instead: end each paragraph except the last on what the next takes up, open the next on it with the plain noun, not a frame ("That question is...", "Those X are why..."), and name any turn there; a stock transition is not a link. Paragraphs that could swap places are a list in disguise: link them or list them.

   Before: ...so the deck hangs from two cables. ¶ Bridges also need anchorages.

   After: ...two cables. ¶ Each cable pulls so hard that its ends sit in concrete anchorages.

6. Close the opening on its claim. Readers measure the whole piece against the opening's last sentence; a memo states its claim first instead, and so do a direct answer and a reference entry.

   Default: a dictionary definition ("A compiler translates source code...") and a slogan, saving the real claim for the end.

   Instead: orient briefly, then end the opening not on a list of topics but on the claim your ending would have reached.

   Before: A heat pump is a device that moves heat from one place to another.

   After: A heat pump moves heat instead of making it, so it warms a house on about a third of the electricity a heater uses.

   Before (memo): This memo reviews our options for the office lease.

   After: We should sign the new lease: it saves $40,000 a year, and only six of us face a longer commute.

7. Replace slogans with claims. Readers take a sentence's last words as its point, and a line that ends on nothing checkable usually marks an unfinished thought.

   Default: quotable lines that sound conclusive ("Roasting is the step that turns raw potential into flavor."), denials set up to be reversed ("Roasting isn't just heating beans. It's chemistry."), and threes by reflex ("brittle, brown, and aromatic", "three key reasons").

   Instead: write the specific claim the line gestures at, or fold its one fact into a neighbor. Never invent a figure to fill it. State a claim directly, and set it against a belief only when this reader holds that belief. List as many items as the material has, two or five as readily as three, and give the one that matters most its own sentence.

   Before: Lighthouses are a triumph of engineering and ingenuity.

   After: A Fresnel lens bends most of a lamp's light into one beam, and ships can see that beam from 30 kilometers out.

   Before: The new process is faster, simpler and more reliable.

   After: The new process cuts approval from three signatures to one, so most requests clear in a day.

8. End each sentence on its news. Readers look to a sentence's opening for its link back, and they feel a claim land only at a period, semicolon or colon.

   Default: locators and tags at the end ("are caught at this stage", "(convection)"), three claims closed by one period (", which is why fires tend to surge"), and stubs that only announce ("Fuel arrangement also matters.", "Here's how it works:", a colon line that only opens a list).

   Instead: open on what the reader holds and close on the new fact, using the passive when only it puts the link first. Give each claim the reader must keep its own sentence, preferring a period to a semicolon or a chain of clauses, and split any sentence past about 30 words. Fold each stub into the sentence carrying its news, and state the point instead of announcing it.

   Before: The client retries a failed request three times, waiting longer each time, which is why a dead server can take 30 seconds to report an error.

   After: The client retries a failed request three times and waits longer before each try. An error from a dead server can therefore take 30 seconds.

   Before: Here's how billing works: you're charged on the first of each month.

   After: Your card is charged on the first of each month.

9. Let clauses rank the facts. Readers weigh two facts by which comes last, holds the main clause and runs longer, read two plain sentences as equals, and split when the cues disagree.

   Default: hedges in every clause and verdict-free pairs ("A faster rise tends to preserve sweetness and fruit, while a slower one builds body and complexity but risks flatness").

   Instead: put the verdict in a main clause at the end ("[lesser fact], but [verdict]"), or give the lesser fact its own sentence just before it. Use "[Verdict], although [concession]" when the two should feel balanced, and open on Although only now and then. Keep each caveat as a placed clause, not a scattered hedge, and never make a warning the concession.

   Before: Desalination can supply drought-proof water, though it tends to need a lot of energy.

   After: Desalination needs enormous amounts of electricity, but it gives a coastal city water no drought can dry up.

   Before (email): We can probably send the draft Friday, though it might slip, depending on how the legal review goes.

   After: We'll send the draft Friday if legal signs off by Thursday noon.

10. Keep the doer in the subject. Readers take whatever opens the main clause as what the sentence is about, look to the verb for the action, and read a new word as a new thing.

    Default: label frames ("The job is to...", "The central constraint is that..."), five openers for one darkening bean, and one fuel called "fuel", "material" and "vegetation".

    Instead: make the doer the subject and its action the verb, keeping "is" for definitions, and hold that subject under one name until the story hands off. Open most sentences on that subject, not on a Because, Although or When clause the reader must hold before learning whose story it is. Once the name is set, let a pronoun carry some sentences, so that three in a row never open on the same word.

    Before: A lock's purpose is lifting boats. The vessel rises as the chamber floods, then the craft motors out.

    After: A lock lifts a boat by flooding its chamber; the boat rises with the water, then motors out.

11. Explain each term where it lands. Readers can use a name, number or mechanism only if they can handle it on arrival.

    Default: bare terms of art ("constant folding, dead code elimination, inlining, loop-invariant code motion") and names in passing ("pyrazines and phenols"); once told to gloss, an appositive on every noun, even words the reader already knows.

    Instead: gloss each term this reader lacks in a few words, at their depth, and keep every term. Put the gloss after the main verb or in its own short sentence; save the appositive for a gloss of two or three words.

    Before: Set a TTL on each cache entry.

    After: Set a TTL on each cache entry. The TTL is the number of seconds an entry lives before the cache discards it.

12. Emphasize by position, not markup. Readers find emphasis in how a sentence is built; markup on top shows the structure failed, and many find it condescending.

    Default: five to ten bold spans per piece ("This is **first crack**") and flag words ("the most important factor").

    Instead: keep bold, italics, capitals, exclamation points, key and crucial out of running prose, and rebuild so the word you would mark closes its sentence. Only a warning the reader must act on may be bold.

    Before: Tread depth is the **most important** factor in a tire's wet grip.

    After: On a wet road, nothing affects a tire's grip more than the depth of its tread.

13. Keep the concrete. Readers grasp an abstract claim through an instance they can picture, and they stop trusting a page that never shows one.

    Default: when told to connect and develop, trading the vivid examples a plain draft would have had for chains of abstract claims, and dropping its practical material.

    Instead: give every abstract claim a specific case in the same or the next sentence (a named thing, a move, a number), and check that the case shows that very claim. One running example may carry several paragraphs. Keep each example that makes an idea stick, and keep the practical material a reader would use, such as normal ranges, deadlines, first steps and what to expect.

    Before: Friction slows moving objects.

    After: Friction is why a hockey puck slides 50 meters on ice but stops within 2 on asphalt.

Final check:

1. Form: each list, table, step and heading does its mandate-2 job, ordered stages are numbered, a standalone piece keeps its title, and no bullet, label or bold carries reasoning (1, 2, 12).
2. Paragraphs: each holds one point, and first and last sentences, read in order, trace one argument from the opening's claim to an ending the reader can use (3, 4, 5, 6).
3. Sentence ends: by eye, not ear, each sentence closes on news, never a locator, tag, slogan, announcement or comma-chained tail, and no three or denial appears by reflex (7, 8).
4. Subjects and clauses: most sentences open on a doer held under one name, no three in a row open alike, and opposed facts end on their verdict (9, 10).
5. Content: every fact, step, number, caveat and practical detail is present with no bracket or blank left for the reader to fill, each term the reader lacks is glossed where it lands, and each abstract claim has a case that shows it (11, 13, preamble).
