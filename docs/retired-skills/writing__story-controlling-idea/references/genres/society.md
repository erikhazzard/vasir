# Society Genre Reference

## Mandatory Genre Contract

> **If Society is the global genre, the controlling idea’s Value must resolve `Impotence / Power`. Philosophy, character arc, setting, and subgenre may determine the Cause or secondary charges; they may not replace this axis.**

| Field | Contract |
|---|---|
| Global value axis | **Impotence / Power** |
| Positive pole | **Power** |
| Negative pole | **Impotence** |
| Core event | The Revolution Scene: the protagonist’s or luminaries’ gifts are expressed or refused and power visibly changes hands—or fails to change hands. |
| Core-event placement | The supplied notes say it can occur anywhere. Operationally, the global ending must establish the final distribution of power. |
| Core emotion | Intrigue |
| Core need | Recognition and effective agency within or against the social hierarchy. |
| Conscious object of desire | Expose hypocrisy and lies of the reigning power, Gain recognition and rights for the disenfranchised, Create social or political change, Overthrow, reform, capture, or redistribute institutional power |

## Value Spectrum — Operational Synthesis

The supplied source names the global poles. The deeper states below operationalize McKee’s contrary, contradictory, and negation-of-negation progression for this genre; tailor the exact wording to the story without leaving the canonical axis.

| Level | Genre state |
|---|---|
| Positive | Power: effective agency, recognition, and capacity to shape the social order |
| Contrary | Dependence, marginal influence, invisibility, or fragmented agency |
| Contradictory | Impotence: inability to affect the hierarchy or protect one’s interests |
| Negation of the negation | Domination presented as empowerment, or a revolution that replaces the tyrant while preserving the machinery of impotence |

## Core Roles — Source-Derived

- **Protagonist / Disenfranchised group:** Seeks recognition, rights, agency, or a new distribution of power.
- **Tyrant / Reigning power:** Maintains control through hierarchy, narrative, coercion, exclusion, or dependence.
- **Rebels / Luminaries:** Allies and contrasting offshoot characters who embody different responses to the central hierarchy.

## Core-Emotion Techniques — Source-Derived

1. Exposing hypocrisy
2. Challenging norms
3. Unveiling secrets
4. Fighting for justice
5. Questioning authority

## Genre Conventions — Source-Derived

- One central character with offshoot characters embodying facets of the central personality or social response.
- A big canvas: broad social system or equally broad internalized social hierarchy.
- A clear revolutionary Point of No Return.
- The vanquished are doomed to exile or exclusion.
- A large power divide between the reigning and disenfranchised.
- A paradoxical, win-but-lose or lose-but-win bittersweet ending.

## Obligatory Moments — Source-Derived, Structurally Normalized

1. A threat to or opportunity for the reigning power incites the protagonist.
2. The protagonist denies responsibility to respond.
3. Forced to respond, the protagonist lashes out according to position in the hierarchy.
4. Each key avatar learns the antagonist’s object of desire.
5. The initial strategy to outmaneuver the antagonist fails.
6. At All Is Lost, the protagonist recognizes a new approach is required to turn the power tables.
7. The Revolution Scene changes—or fails to change—who can act, decide, and control.
8. The protagonist is rewarded or punished at extra-personal, interpersonal, or personal levels.

## Conscious Objects of Desire — Source-Derived / Consolidated

- Expose hypocrisy and lies of the reigning power
- Gain recognition and rights for the disenfranchised
- Create social or political change
- Overthrow, reform, capture, or redistribute institutional power

## Genre Spine

A threat or opportunity exposes the protagonist’s disenfranchisement. The protagonist gathers allies, confronts hypocrisy, and learns the tyrant’s desire. Initial reform or maneuver fails. A betrayal or loss forces a new strategy. The Revolution Scene then changes—or fails to change—the actual control of decisions, resources, recognition, and coercion.

## Twenty-Skeletal-Scene Map — Operational Normalization

The supplied notes often use “climax” for a decision, condition, or encounter. This map enforces:

- **Crisis = choice.**
- **Climax = enacted answer.**
- **Resolution = consequence.**

## Subgenres

### Political Society

- **Selective constraint / focus:** Direct struggle for formal or informal political power; the revolution changes who controls the hierarchy.
- **Supplied examples:** *The Godfather*
- **Value rule:** The global controlling-idea Value remains **Impotence / Power** unless another genre is explicitly designated global. The subgenre changes the force of antagonism, arena, procedures, and likely Cause.

## Adjacent-Genre Disambiguation

- Justice, equality, freedom, truth, and recognition may motivate the revolution; if Society is global the controlling value remains Power/Impotence.
- Choose Western when individual sovereignty and the protector’s showdown—not collective power transfer—control the ending.
- Choose Crime when exposure of wrongdoing is the indispensable payoff and no meaningful power transfer occurs.

## Controlling-Idea Value Rules

- The controlling idea must resolve Power or Impotence.
- Power means effective capacity to act and shape the hierarchy, not merely moral superiority or attention.
- A positive ending must show where power went and how it can be exercised. A negative ending must show why the protagonists remain or become impotent.
- Revolution without changed control is not a positive Society resolution.
