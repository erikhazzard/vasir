## Core Roles — Source-Derived

- **Lovers:** Seek or resist love and must become capable of vulnerability, commitment, and integration.
- **Rival:** A person, ethic, duty, social system, habit, or fear that competes with the relationship.
- **Confidant:** Provides perspective, encouragement, warning, or pressure.
- **Helpers and Harmers:** Characters and forces actively supporting or opposing the match.

## Core-Emotion Techniques — Source-Derived

1. Romantic tension and release
2. Emotional vulnerability
3. Sacrifice for love
4. Attraction and repulsion
5. Intimacy and conflict

## Genre Conventions — Source-Derived

- A relationship triangle that includes a Rival; the rival may be a person or competing ethic/duty.
- Helpers and Harmers who actively support or oppose the relationship.
- Ordered and chaotic approaches to love and life that must integrate or remain incompatible.
- An external need outside the romance driving action.
- Internal or external forces opposing the match.
- Secrets: society from couple, couple from society, lovers from one another, or a lover from self.
- Rituals of intimacy such as private language, repeated places, shared acts, and traditions.
- Moral weight: inability to love is tied to a flaw that must be confronted for a positive ending.

## Obligatory Moments — Source-Derived, Structurally Normalized

1. Lovers meet or become newly aware of one another.
2. First kiss or intimate connection acknowledges attraction or care.
3. Confession of love exposes vulnerability.
4. Lovers break up or are forced apart.
5. Proof of love: sacrifice without assurance of reward.
6. Lovers reunite and commit—or fail/refuse to reunite, establishing Estrangement/Hate.

## Conscious Objects of Desire — Source-Derived / Consolidated

- Win the beloved’s affection
- Overcome internal and external barriers to be together
- Achieve mutual understanding and respect
- Secure a shared future or meaningful commitment

## Genre Spine

The relationship evolves through attraction, intimacy, secrets, opposition, rupture, and proof. External demands and internal flaws make connection costly. The ending tests whether one or both lovers can sacrifice without guarantee, thereby proving love—or whether fear, possession, pride, deceit, or incompatible duty produces estrangement.

## Adjacent-Genre Disambiguation

- If survival from an obsessive pursuer or monstrous beloved controls the ending, Thriller or Horror may be global and Love secondary.
- If public performance or achievement controls the ending, Performance may be global even when romance is central to a subplot.
- For a tragic romance, do not assume a negative Love charge merely because a lover dies; a death can prove Love while producing a negative Life charge in a different global genre.

## Controlling-Idea Value Rules

- Choose one consistent expression of the axis: Love/Hate or Intimacy/Estrangement. Do not switch casually inside one controlling idea.
- Desire, marriage, sex, social acceptance, authenticity, and self-love may be causes or secondary values; they do not replace the global Love value.
- The ending must include a Proof of Love or a decisive failure/refusal/counterfeit of that proof.
